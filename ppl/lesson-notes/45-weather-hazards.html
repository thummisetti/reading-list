Lesson 45 — Weather Hazards

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The lesson gathers the weather phenomena most likely to turn a routine light-aircraft flight unsafe: inversions/poor visibility, fog, turbulence, icing, thunderstorms, wind shear/microbursts, plus the existing performance concern of high density altitude.
  • The recurring strategy is recognize early and avoid. Many of these hazards cannot be safely “handled” once a low-time VFR pilot is deeply committed to them.

Temperature inversions

  • Normally temperature decreases with height through much of the lower atmosphere; an inversion is a layer where temperature instead increases with altitude.
  • Stable inversion layers suppress vertical mixing and can trap haze, smoke, moisture, and pollutants near the surface, reducing visibility.
  • Inversions can also mark abrupt wind changes and contribute to wind shear/turbulence near their boundaries.

Fog / low visibility

  • Fog is a surface-based cloud produced when near-surface air reaches saturation.
  • Radiation, advection, upslope, and evaporation/steam mechanisms create fog under different combinations of moisture, wind, terrain, and cooling.
  • A shrinking temperature/dew-point spread is a warning clue, not a guarantee that fog will form.
  • VFR pilots should treat forecast lowering ceilings/visibility conservatively and retain diversion/turn-around options before visual references disappear.

Turbulence

  • Turbulence can be caused by convection, fronts, terrain/mechanical mixing, strong wind gradients, and wake vortices.
  • Severity depends partly on aircraft size/speed/loading. Use PIREPs with aircraft type and altitude context.
  • In significant turbulence, use the aircraft's recommended turbulence-penetration procedure/speed and avoid abrupt control inputs; do not assume maneuvering speed is a universal “turbulence-proof” number.

Icing

  • Structural icing requires supercooled liquid water/visible moisture and a sufficiently cold aircraft surface.
  • Ice changes the airfoil, increases drag/weight, reduces lift, can increase stall speed, and can affect propeller, windshield, antennas, pitot/static/induction systems.
  • Common forms include rime, clear, and mixed icing; freezing rain can produce especially rapid accumulation.
  • Carburetor icing is a separate induction-system hazard and can occur at temperatures well above freezing under suitable humidity/temperature conditions.
  • An aircraft not approved/equipped for icing should avoid known/forecast icing environments rather than planning to “pick up a little.”

Thunderstorms

  • Require moisture + instability + lifting action.
  • Life cycle: cumulus/developing stage dominated by updrafts; mature stage with updrafts and downdrafts/precipitation; dissipating stage dominated by downdrafts.
  • Hazards include severe/extreme turbulence, hail, lightning, heavy rain, icing, tornadoes, gust fronts, wind shear, and powerful up/downdrafts.
  • Do not fly beneath or through thunderstorms in a light airplane. Use generous avoidance and current FAA/weather guidance; embedded cells are especially dangerous because visual avoidance is impossible.

Wind shear and microbursts

  • Wind shear is a rapid change in wind speed/direction over a short distance.
  • A microburst is an intense localized downdraft that spreads outward near the surface.
  • On approach/departure, an aircraft can encounter increasing headwind, downdraft, then rapidly increasing tailwind—creating severe performance/airspeed changes close to the ground.
  • If microburst/wind-shear alerts or credible reports affect the runway path, avoidance/delay/diversion is normally the safest response.

Density altitude

  • High temperature, high pressure altitude, and lower air density reduce normally aspirated engine power, propeller thrust, and aerodynamic performance.
  • Expect longer takeoff roll, lower climb rate/angle, and reduced margin over obstacles.
  • Humidity has a smaller but directionally adverse effect on density than temperature/pressure. Use the actual POH performance charts.

Weather hazards interact

  • Do not evaluate hazards one at a time. A hot, gusty high-density-altitude day with terrain and convective buildup can be unacceptable even though no single number exceeds a regulatory limit.
  • Combine forecasts, observations, PIREPs, advisories, radar/satellite/GFA, terrain, and personal minimums.

What to remember

  • Inversions can trap haze/moisture and produce shear.
  • Fog/low visibility: avoid losing visual reference.
  • Icing dramatically changes performance and systems.
  • Thunderstorm = avoid, not penetrate.
  • Microbursts are especially dangerous close to the ground.
  • High density altitude can erase takeoff/climb margin.
