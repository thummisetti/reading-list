Lesson 26 — VFR Sectional — Altitudes

Core idea

  • A sectional shows terrain, man-made obstacles, airport elevations, airspace limits, and Maximum Elevation Figures (MEFs) so pilots can build vertical situational awareness.
  • Always distinguish MSL (elevation referenced to mean sea level) from AGL (height above the local ground).
  • Chart information supports altitude planning, but legal minimum-altitude rules and operational terrain clearance are separate questions.

Obstacle symbols and numbers

  • Sectionals depict significant man-made obstacles such as towers. Use the current chart legend for exact depiction/charting criteria.
  • For a charted obstacle, the upper/bold number gives the elevation of the top in feet MSL.
  • The lower number in parentheses gives the obstacle's height AGL.
  • Lesson example: top at 1,343 ft MSL, obstacle height 275 ft AGL.
  • If both values are known, approximate ground elevation at the obstacle = top elevation MSL − obstacle height AGL.
  • A different, emphasized symbol is used for very tall obstacles; the lesson highlights towers more than 1,000 ft AGL.
  • A group-obstacle symbol can stand for multiple nearby obstacles. The listed values correspond to the controlling/tallest obstacle in the group, not necessarily every object shown.
  • In cluttered chart areas the AGL height may be omitted and only the top elevation shown.

Do not infer exact tower height from a nearby airport elevation

  • The video suggests subtracting a nearby airport's field elevation from an obstacle's top elevation if an AGL height is omitted.
  • That can be a rough written-test approximation only when the problem setup supports it. Terrain elevation at the tower may differ materially from airport elevation.
  • For operational planning, use actual charted terrain/obstacle data rather than treating the nearest airport elevation as the tower-base elevation.

Wind farms, unverified data, and lighting

  • Dense wind-turbine areas may be depicted collectively, with the elevation of the tallest controlling turbine rather than individual labels for every turbine.
  • The lesson points out a chart notation indicating an obstacle is under construction or its top elevation is unverified. Treat such values conservatively and consult current chart/NOTAM data.
  • A lightning-bolt-style marking on an obstacle symbol indicates high-intensity lighting in the lesson's chart legend.

§91.119 minimum-safe-altitude concepts reviewed in the lesson

  • Everywhere: except when necessary for takeoff or landing, the airplane must be high enough that an engine failure would permit an emergency landing without undue hazard to persons or property on the surface.
  • Congested areas: at least 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a horizontal radius of 2,000 ft of the aircraft.
  • Other than congested areas: generally at least 500 ft above the surface.
  • Over open water or sparsely populated areas: the aircraft may be below 500 ft above the surface, but may not be operated closer than 500 ft to any person, vessel, vehicle, or structure.
  • The instructor uses a worked example with an obstacle top at 2,758 ft MSL; adding 1,000 ft gives 3,758 ft MSL as the minimum height over that obstacle under the congested-area rule, assuming the aircraft is within the specified horizontal radius.

Yellow city shading is not a legal definition of “congested”

  • The lesson uses yellow populated-area shading as a conservative practical cue for treating an area as congested.
  • That is useful planning conservatism, but the chart tint itself does not create or conclusively define the legal §91.119 “congested area.”

Maximum Elevation Figure (MEF)

  • MEFs are the large blue numbers printed inside sectional quadrangles bounded by lines of latitude/longitude.
  • They provide a conservative charted indication of the highest terrain/obstacle elevation in that quadrangle, with allowance/rounding built into the charting method.
  • The lesson walks through two construction concepts: one where a man-made obstacle is controlling and another where natural terrain is controlling.
  • In the obstacle example, the instructor starts from a 1,904-ft-MSL obstacle, applies allowance/rounding, and arrives at an MEF of 2,100 ft.
  • When terrain is controlling, the charting process includes additional allowance for possible uncharted obstacles and vertical error/uncertainty before rounding.

MEF is not a guaranteed “safe altitude”

  • The video says that flying over the MEF should keep you safe from towers/obstructions in the quadrangle. Do not turn that sentence into an operating rule.
  • MEF is not a regulatory minimum altitude, does not incorporate every operational margin you may need, and by itself does not guarantee terrain/obstacle clearance under all conditions.
  • Choose an altitude using current charts, terrain/obstacle information, §91.119, airspace, weather, aircraft performance, route-specific margins, and any other applicable constraints.

Airspace vertical limits

  • Class B shelf numbers are charted in hundreds of feet MSL: e.g. 100/40 means 10,000 ft MSL ceiling over a 4,000 ft MSL floor.
  • Class C/D ceilings and other charted vertical limits likewise require careful attention to units and whether the lower limit is surface or an altitude.
  • Class E transition-area symbology identifies common bases such as surface, 700 AGL, or 1,200 AGL; use the current chart legend and surrounding depiction to determine the actual base.

VFR cruising altitudes

  • When §91.159 applies—generally level cruising flight more than 3,000 ft above the surface—magnetic course determines the VFR altitude series.
  • Magnetic course 0°–179°: odd thousands + 500 ft; 180°–359°: even thousands + 500 ft, subject to airspace and other restrictions.

What to remember

  • Obstacle top elevation = MSL; parenthetical height = AGL.
  • Group symbols may represent more obstacles than the drawing suggests.
  • Do not equate yellow populated-area shading with the legal definition of congested area.
  • Know the §91.119 1,000/2,000-ft congested rule and 500-ft other-area rules.
  • MEF is a planning clue, not a legal or guaranteed safe altitude.
  • Read all chart vertical values with their proper MSL/AGL context.
