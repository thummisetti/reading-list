Lesson 54 — Privileges & Limitations

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • A private pilot certificate gives broad personal-transport and passenger-carrying privileges within the pilot's ratings, endorsements, currency, medical qualification, aircraft limitations, and operating rules.
  • The central limitation in §61.113 is that a private pilot generally may not act as PIC carrying persons/property for compensation or hire, nor act as PIC for compensation or hire, except for specifically defined exceptions.
  • The instructor's checkride-study point is important: know where the rule lives—§61.113—and do not reduce the entire subject to “private pilots cannot be paid.”

Certificate privileges are not universal aircraft privileges

  • A private certificate does not authorize every aircraft. You still need the appropriate category/class rating, type rating where required, and endorsements for operations such as tailwheel, complex, high-performance, or pressurized/high-altitude aircraft where applicable.
  • Instrument privileges require the appropriate instrument rating and currency when operating under IFR or below VFR minimums.
  • The aircraft itself must be legal and the operation must comply with the applicable part 91 rules.

Passenger carrying and currency

  • Passenger carriage requires the applicable recent takeoff-and-landing experience under §61.57.
  • Night passenger carriage has its own night takeoff/landing recency conditions; do not confuse the regulatory definition used for passenger currency with other definitions of “night” used elsewhere.
  • A flight review or qualifying alternative is also required on the applicable cycle to act as PIC.

Incidental business flying — §61.113(b)

  • A private pilot may act as PIC in connection with a business or employment for compensation/hire only when the flight is incidental to that business or employment and the aircraft does not carry passengers or property for compensation or hire.
  • The key distinction is that the pilot is not being employed primarily to provide air transportation.

Expense sharing — §61.113(c)

  • A private pilot may share specified operating expenses with passengers but may not pay less than the pilot's pro rata share.
  • The regulation limits shareable expenses to fuel, oil, airport expenditures, and rental fees.
  • Current FAA guidance also requires a bona fide common purpose between pilot and passengers, with the pilot having an independent reason for the destination.
  • A pilot cannot use expense sharing as a way to offer transportation to the public. Advertising or other “holding out” can turn the activity into a commercial/common-carriage problem.

Other explicit exceptions in §61.113

  • Charitable, nonprofit, or community-event flights may be conducted when the requirements of §91.146 and §61.113 are met.
  • Specified search-and-location operations may permit reimbursement of limited operating expenses when sanctioned and controlled by an eligible agency/organization.
  • An aircraft salesperson with at least 200 hours of logged flight time may demonstrate an aircraft in flight to a prospective buyer under the stated rule.
  • Qualifying pilots may conduct glider/unpowered-ultralight towing under §61.69, and §61.113 contains additional narrowly defined exceptions.
  • Each exception has conditions; never generalize one exception into a broad right to receive compensation.

BasicMed relationship

  • Current §61.113(i) also contains the operating conditions for exercising qualifying privileges under BasicMed.
  • BasicMed changes the medical-qualification path; it does not remove the underlying private-pilot compensation/ratings/currency limitations.

Checkride framing

  • When asked “Can a private pilot be paid?”, the best answer is not simply yes or no. Start with the general prohibition, then identify the applicable exception and its exact conditions.
  • For expense sharing, remember three separate ideas: allowed expense categories + pro rata share + common purpose/no holding out.

What to remember

  • Private pilot: broad personal/passenger privileges, generally no compensation/hire.
  • Incidental-business exception has strict conditions.
  • Expense sharing: fuel/oil/airport/rental only; pilot pays at least pro rata; common purpose; no holding out.
  • Other exceptions are specific—charitable/community flights, sanctioned search/location reimbursement, qualifying aircraft sales demonstrations, towing, etc.
  • Ratings, endorsements, recent experience, flight review, medical qualification, aircraft limitations, and operating rules all still apply.
