Lesson 23 — What Is Zulu Time?

Comprehensive transcript-merged study notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course

Core idea

  • Aviation needs one common time reference because a flight can depart one time zone and arrive in another, and local clocks can also change for daylight saving time.
  • UTC (Coordinated Universal Time), commonly called Zulu time in aviation, gives pilots, ATC, weather services, and other agencies an unambiguous common clock.
  • Zulu time itself does not shift for daylight saving time.

24-hour clock

  • Aviation normally expresses Zulu time with a 24-hour clock and no AM/PM.
  • Midnight begins the day at 0000; 0100 = 1 a.m.; 0800 = 8 a.m.; noon = 1200.
  • For afternoon/evening local times, add 12 to the hour: 1 p.m. = 1300, 3 p.m. = 1500, 11 p.m. = 2300.
  • The final minute of the day is 2359, followed by 0000 on the next date.
  • In common aviation weather/date-time use, think in the 0000–2359 sequence. The video's blanket statement that “2400 does not exist” is broader than necessary; some non-aviation standards permit 2400 to denote the end of a day.

U.S. local time → Zulu

  • The lesson's standard-time offsets are: EST +5, CST +6, MST +7, PST +8.
  • During daylight saving time the offsets become: EDT +4, CDT +5, MDT +6, PDT +7.
  • To convert local to Zulu in the contiguous U.S., first identify whether the location is on standard or daylight time, convert the local time to 24-hour notation, then add the correct offset.
  • If the sum passes 2400, subtract 24 hours and advance the Zulu calendar date by one day.
  • For Zulu → local, reverse the offset and account for any date change.

Daylight-saving caveat

  • The video says Arizona and Hawaii do not observe daylight saving time. Hawaii does not, and most of Arizona does not; however, the Navajo Nation in Arizona observes DST.
  • For real flight planning, use the time convention applicable to the actual location and date rather than memorizing a state-wide shortcut.

Worked examples from the lesson

  • Mountain Standard Time: 11 a.m. local = 1100; add 7 hours → 1800Z.
  • Georgia during daylight saving time: 1 p.m. local = 1300 EDT. Add 4 hours → 1700Z departure. A two-hour flight lands at 1900Z.
  • The instructor emphasizes that this conversion style is common written-test material and that the FAA testing supplement provides conversion references.

Zulu time in aviation documents

  • Weather reports/forecasts, NOTAMs, and many operational publications use Zulu so everyone reads the same effective time regardless of location.
  • The Chart Supplement commonly publishes operating hours in Zulu.
  • In the example shown in the video, a “++” notation indicates that daylight-saving effective hours are one hour earlier in Zulu than the printed standard-time hours. The local operating hours remain the same while the UTC offset changes.
  • Always read the publication's legend/remarks rather than assuming the notation from memory.

Date-time groups

  • Zulu times are often embedded in a date-time group: day of month followed by hour/minute and the letter Z.
  • Example pattern: 131900Z means the 13th day of the month at 1900 UTC.
  • Because the reference is UTC, the Zulu date can differ from the local calendar date around midnight.

What to remember

  • UTC and Zulu are the common aviation time reference.
  • Zulu does not change for DST; the local-to-Zulu offset does.
  • 24-hour clock: 0000 through 2359; no AM/PM.
  • Standard-time U.S. offsets: +5 / +6 / +7 / +8 from Eastern through Pacific; daylight offsets are one hour smaller.
  • Watch the date when a conversion crosses midnight.
  • Read Chart Supplement and weather-product legends for their exact time notation.
