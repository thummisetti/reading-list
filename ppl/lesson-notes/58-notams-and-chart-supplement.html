Lesson 58 — NOTAMs & the Chart Supplement

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • §91.103 requires the PIC to become familiar with available information concerning the flight. That includes time-sensitive changes even for a short local flight or pattern session.
  • Charts and the Chart Supplement describe the published baseline; NOTAMs describe temporary, abnormal, or recently changed conditions that can make that baseline wrong for today.
  • The transcript's opening joke makes the practical point: “I'm only doing patterns” is not a reason to skip current-information checks.

What a NOTAM does

  • A NOTAM provides essential aeronautical information that is temporary or was not known far enough in advance to publish through the normal chart/publication cycle.
  • Common subjects include runway/taxiway closures, construction, lighting outages, navaid or communication outages, procedure changes, hazards, airspace restrictions, and other abnormal National Airspace System conditions.
  • Read a NOTAM for what facility/location it affects, what changed, when it is effective, and whether the change matters to your route/operation.
  • Effective times are commonly expressed in UTC/Zulu, so convert carefully and watch the calendar date when crossing midnight.

Useful NOTAM categories

  • NOTAM(D) commonly covers information about airports, facilities, navaids, services, procedures, and hazards that is distributed beyond the local facility.
  • FDC NOTAMs commonly contain regulatory flight information such as amendments to published instrument procedures or airspace information.
  • Center-area, military, international, and other classifications exist, but for a private pilot the operational question is more important than taxonomy: does this NOTAM change what I can safely or legally do?

Route-wide NOTAM thinking

  • Do not check only the destination airport identifier. Review departure, destination, alternates/diversion airports, relevant navaids, airspace/TFR information, and route facilities.
  • A VOR or communication facility serving your route may be affected even when the airport itself has no obvious NOTAM.
  • Recheck time-sensitive information close to departure because conditions can change after the first briefing.

Chart Supplement — what it is

  • The Chart Supplement is the FAA's detailed facility directory; older pilots/material may call it the Airport/Facility Directory (A/FD).
  • It is issued on a 56-day cycle and is organized regionally in the U.S.
  • It carries information that cannot fit cleanly on a sectional: detailed runway data, communications, services, fuel, lighting, weather sources, navaids, remarks, hours, diagrams, special notices, and other facility information.

Airport entry workflow

  • Confirm airport identifier, location/elevation, runway dimensions/surfaces, lighting, and available approaches/services as applicable.
  • Review communication frequencies and identify the relevant tower, CTAF, approach/departure, center, Flight Service, clearance-delivery, or remote communication information.
  • Check fuel types, service availability, and operating hours before depending on them. The transcript gives the practical example of avoiding arrival after fuel service closes.
  • Read remarks for items such as noise-abatement procedures, unusual runway conditions, special traffic notes, or equipment not obvious from the sectional.

Unusual airport features

  • The video uses runway arresting/barrier equipment as an example of why remarks and symbols matter. A pilot unfamiliar with a feature should not guess how it operates or whether it affects the usable runway.
  • Use the current publication and NOTAMs, and ask ATC/airport personnel when appropriate. The broader lesson is to resolve ambiguity before committing to the operation.

Communications and controlling facility

  • The Chart Supplement can help identify which ATC facility serves an airport/area and which frequencies are appropriate.
  • The transcript notes the line-of-sight nature of VHF communications: terrain and low altitude can limit radio range, while a higher safe altitude or another published frequency may improve contact.
  • Do not treat a frequency example from the video as permanent; frequencies and facility assignments can change, so use current data.

Use the sources together

  • Sectional/chart: geographical and airspace baseline.
  • Chart Supplement: detailed published facility information.
  • NOTAMs/TFR information: temporary/current changes.
  • Weather products/briefing: current and forecast atmospheric conditions.
  • A complete preflight picture comes from reconciling all of them rather than trusting any one source alone.

Current terminology correction

  • Current FAA terminology is Notice to Airmen (NOTAM). On February 10, 2025, the FAA changed the expansion back from “Notice to Air Missions.”
  • Older course material may use “Notice to Air Missions”; treat that as historical wording, not the current FAA expansion.

What to remember

  • §91.103: check available information pertinent to the flight—even for local flying.
  • NOTAM = temporary/recent abnormal information that can supersede the published baseline.
  • Read location, condition, effective time, and operational impact.
  • Chart Supplement = detailed airport/facility data, updated on a 56-day cycle.
  • Read remarks; do not guess about unfamiliar airport features.
  • Combine chart + Chart Supplement + NOTAM/TFR + weather, and recheck near departure.
