Lesson 48 — The 60:1 Rule

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • This lesson uses the fact that there are 60 minutes in an hour to make fast time, speed, distance, and fuel calculations without an E6B.
  • The same mental habit is useful in flight because it turns hourly rates into per-minute rates that are easy to multiply.
  • The lesson’s practical goal is not just passing a written test—it is being able to make quick fuel/time decisions when plans change in the airplane.

Speed → distance per minute

  • If groundspeed is in knots, divide by 60 to get nautical miles traveled per minute.
  • 60 kt = 1 NM/min; 90 kt = 1.5 NM/min; 120 kt = 2 NM/min.
  • Then multiply the per-minute rate by elapsed minutes.
  • Transcript example: at 90 kt for 70 minutes, 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5 NM/min; 1.5 × 70 = 105 NM.
  • Use groundspeed—not indicated or true airspeed—when calculating actual distance over the ground.

Distance ↔ time

  • Distance = groundspeed × time.
  • Time = distance ÷ groundspeed.
  • For mental math, convert groundspeed to NM/min first. Example: at 120 kt, 30 NM takes about 15 minutes.
  • For a rough reasonableness check, ask whether the result matches the simple 60-knot baseline: 60 kt covers 60 NM in one hour.

Fuel per minute

  • Start with the expected fuel flow from the POH/AFM or a known operational value.
  • Divide gallons per hour by 60 to get gallons per minute, then multiply by minutes flown.
  • Transcript example: 7.5 GPH ÷ 60 = 0.125 gal/min. For 43 minutes, fuel used is about 5.4 gallons.
  • The same method works backward: usable fuel divided by fuel-per-minute gives approximate endurance.

Fuel reserve decisions

  • The transcript works an in-flight example comparing fuel remaining, expected burn to destination, and the daytime VFR reserve.
  • At an 8.4 GPH burn, 30 minutes of fuel is 4.2 gallons. The example reaches the destination with only slightly more than that.
  • The instructor’s point is important: being barely legal is not a good planning target. Wind, routing, mixture, taxi time, weather, or an unexpected delay can erase a thin reserve.
  • Use the legal reserve as a floor and choose a larger personal reserve when practical.

Wind and course correction

  • The lesson also extends quick mental math to in-flight navigation and changing winds.
  • Compare actual groundspeed/checkpoint time with the plan. If you are consistently early or late, recompute ETA and fuel rather than continuing with stale numbers.
  • For lateral navigation, the classic angular 60:1 approximation is also useful: about 1° of track error produces 1 NM of displacement after 60 NM.
  • Track-error angle ≈ off-course distance ÷ distance flown × 60. A closing angle can be estimated the same way using distance remaining to the desired intercept point.

Accuracy note

  • The transcript’s main “60” technique is the 60-minutes-per-hour rate conversion. The angular 60:1 navigation rule is a related small-angle approximation, not the reason hourly fuel/speed conversions work.
  • Use exact POH/AFM fuel and performance data for planning; mental math is a cross-check and an in-flight decision aid.

What to remember

  • Knots ÷ 60 = NM per minute.
  • GPH ÷ 60 = gallons per minute.
  • Distance = speed × time; fuel = flow × time.
  • Update ETA and fuel when actual progress differs from the nav log.
  • A legal reserve is not necessarily a comfortable reserve.
  • Angular 60:1: 1° ≈ 1 NM at 60 NM.
