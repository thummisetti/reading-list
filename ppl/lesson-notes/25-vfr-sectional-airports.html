Lesson 25 — VFR Sectional — Airports

Comprehensive transcript-merged study notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course

Core idea

  • A VFR sectional compresses a large amount of airport information into symbols, colors, labels, and abbreviations.
  • Use the Aeronautical Chart User's Guide and Chart Supplement whenever a symbol or operating detail is uncertain. The lesson repeatedly models this habit.
  • Read the airport symbol together with nearby airspace, terrain, obstacles, frequencies, and current NOTAMs rather than treating the airport symbol in isolation.

Basic airport symbols

  • A basic magenta circle can represent an airport with other-than-hard-surface runways such as grass/dirt, or a short hard-surface runway that is not depicted to scale.
  • An empty basic airport circle indicates public use in the examples shown.
  • H identifies a heliport; F identifies an ultralight flight park; U indicates unverified airport information.
  • R identifies a restricted-use/private-use airport in the lesson's chart examples; prior permission is normally needed before using a private field except as justified by an emergency.
  • An airport symbol with an X identifies an abandoned airport.
  • The word OBJECTIONABLE signals an FAA objection associated with that airport/site. Do not infer the exact hazard from the word alone—consult current chart/publication data.

Abandoned airports are not automatic emergency runways

  • The instructor notes that long abandoned runways may look attractive as emergency-landing options.
  • That is useful situational awareness, but an abandoned runway may be obstructed, deteriorated, closed, repurposed, crossed by wires/vehicles, or otherwise unusable. Treat the chart symbol as a clue, not a guarantee of suitability.

Hard-surface runway depictions

  • When chart scale permits, hard-surface runways are depicted in their approximate orientation instead of only as a simple circle.
  • The lesson distinguishes smaller hard-surface-airport symbols from large-airport depictions where runway outlines are more prominent.
  • Do not rely on memorized historical size thresholds alone; chart-symbol specifications can change. Use the current Chart User's Guide for the exact depiction standard.

Fuel, beacon, and airport type clues

  • Tick marks around an airport symbol are used in the lesson as a cue that fuel services are available. For a real fuel stop, verify availability/hours because small airports can temporarily run out or close services.
  • A star associated with the airport symbol indicates a rotating beacon.
  • Airport beacons help locate an airport at night and can help identify airport type by flash colors.
  • Lesson patterns: civilian land airport = alternating white/green; military = green + two quick white; heliport = white/yellow/green; water airport/seaplane base = white/yellow.
  • Some airports may operate a beacon during daylight when weather is below basic VFR conditions. This is a useful warning clue but not a substitute for an actual weather report.

Blue vs. magenta

  • Blue airport information/symbols generally indicate a towered airport; magenta generally indicates a non-towered airport.
  • A control tower can be part-time. The symbol/data block and Chart Supplement must be read together to determine whether the tower is actually operating at your time of flight.

Other symbols near airports

  • A small dot on/near the airport depiction may show the approximate location of an on-field VOR, VOR/DME, or VORTAC; identify the facility from its navaid data box.
  • An anchor symbol identifies a seaplane base.

Reading the airport data block

  • Typical chart data include the airport name and location identifier.
  • Tower/CTAF and weather-reporting frequencies may be printed near the airport. The lesson shows a part-time tower example where an asterisk directs the pilot to operating-hour details.
  • When the tower is closed, a published frequency may serve as the CTAF; verify the current Chart Supplement entry instead of assuming all fields use the same arrangement.
  • Weather may be supplied by ATIS, AWOS, ASOS, or another service; tune the frequency shown for that airport.
  • Airport field elevation is shown in feet MSL.
  • An L notation is used for airport lighting from sunset to sunrise; an accompanying asterisk can indicate limitations that must be checked in the Chart Supplement.
  • Longest runway length is given in hundreds of feet in the compact airport data.
  • RP followed by runway numbers identifies runways using a right-hand traffic pattern rather than the standard left pattern.
  • AOE means Airport of Entry and indicates customs/border-entry capability in the lesson example; verify current entry procedures before international operations.

Currency and verification

  • Printed chart data can lag temporary conditions. Check current NOTAMs for runway closures, lighting outages, frequency/service changes, taxiway restrictions, and other temporary information.
  • For planned services such as fuel, maintenance, customs, or after-hours access, verify directly or through current authoritative airport data.
  • The best exam habit and real-world habit are the same: decode—don't guess.

What to remember

  • Blue generally towered; magenta generally non-towered.
  • Read both the airport symbol and its data block.
  • Know basic special symbols: H, F, U, R, X, rotating-beacon star, seaplane-base anchor.
  • Fuel/beacon/lighting symbols are clues; verify current availability and limitations.
  • RP identifies right traffic; AOE identifies an airport of entry.
  • Use the Chart User's Guide + Chart Supplement + NOTAMs rather than relying on memory alone.
