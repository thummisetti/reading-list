Lesson 15 — Types of Airspace

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript, the existing study notes, and current FAA airspace guidance.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Airspace knowledge is not just memorizing shapes. For every area ask: what class/type is this, where are its boundaries, may I enter VFR, what communication/equipment is required, and what weather minimums apply?

Core framework

  • The FAA groups airspace into four broad types: controlled, uncontrolled, special use, and other airspace.
  • The six familiar classes are A, B, C, D, E, and G.
  • A through E are controlled airspace. Class G is uncontrolled airspace.
  • “Controlled” does not mean every VFR airplane is continuously being vectored by ATC. It means ATC services and regulatory requirements exist according to the class.
  • The lesson's purpose is an overview; later lessons go deeper on B, C, D, E, G, special-use, and other airspace.

Why airspace is divided into classes

  • Different locations have different traffic density, airport activity, IFR operations, hazards, and need for separation/services.
  • The airspace class determines items such as entry/communication rules, equipment, ATC services, and VFR weather minimums.
  • As a VFR pilot, you remain responsible for chart awareness, regulatory compliance, and see-and-avoid even when receiving radar advisories.

Class A — high-altitude controlled airspace

  • Class A generally begins at 18,000 ft MSL and extends up to and including FL600.
  • FL600 corresponds to a pressure-altitude flight level of 60,000 ft with the altimeter set to the standard setting used for flight levels.
  • Operations in Class A are generally IFR only; a private pilot flying ordinary VFR training operations will not enter it.
  • Class A is not specially outlined on VFR sectionals the way B/C/D are because its vertical limits are nationally standardized.

Class B — busiest major airports

  • Class B surrounds many of the nation's busiest airports and is individually tailored to contain major arrival/departure flows.
  • The familiar shape is an upside-down wedding cake: a surface core plus shelves that extend farther outward at higher altitudes.
  • It generally extends from the surface in the core to approximately 10,000 ft MSL, but each Class B's actual boundaries are charted and individually tailored.
  • VFR pilots need an explicit ATC clearance to enter Class B — hearing your call sign is not by itself the Class B clearance.
  • The later Class B lesson covers certification, equipment, mode C/ADS-B, weather, and clearance details.

Class C — radar/towered medium-busy airports

  • Class C generally surrounds airports with an operating tower and radar approach control meeting traffic criteria.
  • Typical geometry is a 5-NM-radius surface core plus a 10-NM-radius shelf, extending to about 4,000 ft above airport elevation (charted in MSL).
  • The transcript uses the memory aid “C for crowded”.
  • Before entry, the pilot must establish two-way radio communication with the controlling ATC facility; the exact communications standard matters and is covered in Lesson 17.
  • Transponder/altitude reporting and ADS-B Out requirements generally apply unless ATC authorizes otherwise.

Class D — smaller towered airports

  • Class D generally surrounds an airport with an operating control tower.
  • It normally extends from the surface to approximately 2,500 ft above airport elevation, with the ceiling charted in MSL; actual lateral dimensions are individually tailored.
  • The transcript uses “D for dialogue”: establish two-way radio communication with the tower before entering.
  • Part-time Class D can revert to Class E or Class G when the tower closes; the Chart Supplement tells you the status/times.

Class E — controlled airspace “everywhere else”

  • Class E is controlled airspace that is not A, B, C, or D.
  • The transcript's useful mnemonic is “E for everywhere else.”
  • In many areas Class E begins at 1,200 ft AGL; around many airports/routes it begins at 700 ft AGL, and it can also be designated to the surface.
  • It normally extends upward until the base of Class A at 18,000 ft MSL, with additional high-altitude Class E above FL600 and in other designated areas.
  • Ordinary VFR flight generally does not require contacting ATC before entering Class E, but VFR weather minimums and other regulations still apply.
  • The sectional-chart boundary symbology is critical and is covered in the dedicated Class E lesson.

Class G — uncontrolled airspace

  • Class G is the portion of airspace that has not been designated A, B, C, D, or E.
  • The transcript uses the mnemonic “G for government free,” but this is only a memory device: federal regulations still apply in Class G.
  • ATC does not provide the same controlled-airspace separation structure there, and aircraft may be outside radar/radio coverage.
  • VFR cloud/visibility minimums in G vary substantially with altitude and day/night, so do not memorize a single Class G weather rule.

Accuracy notes on the overview

  • The transcript casually equates “controlled airspace” with being under “radar control.” That is too broad. Controlled-airspace status does not guarantee radar coverage or mean every VFR airplane is being controlled.
  • The transcript gives simplified dimensions such as a Class D “5-mile diameter.” Actual Class D shapes are individually tailored; use the chart rather than treating a generic diameter as a boundary.
  • Likewise, the common B/C “wedding-cake” dimensions are memory aids/typical configurations, not substitutes for the published chart.

Hierarchy when airspace overlaps

  • When classifications overlap, the rules of the more restrictive airspace apply.
  • FAA hierarchy for this purpose: A > B > C > D > E > G.
  • Special-use or other restrictions may impose additional limits regardless of the underlying class.

Basic VFR weather pattern

  • Class B: 3 statute miles visibility and clear of clouds.
  • Classes C, D, and E below 10,000 ft MSL: generally 3 SM visibility, 500 ft below / 1,000 ft above / 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds — “3-152.”
  • Class E at or above 10,000 ft MSL: generally 5 SM, 1,000 below / 1,000 above / 1 SM horizontal.
  • Class G: varies by altitude and day/night; use the current §91.155 / AIM weather-minimum table.
  • Student pilots have additional visibility/cloud restrictions under their own operating limitations; basic VFR minima are not automatically the only standard that matters.

Worth watching — the 3-D airspace picture

  • The overview graphic showing A above everything, B/C/D around airports, E filling much of the remaining controlled airspace, and G near the surface is worth seeing once.
  • The B/C “wedding cake” shapes and the transition from G to E are much easier to retain visually than from definitions alone.

What to remember for review

  • Four broad types: controlled, uncontrolled, special use, other.
  • Controlled = A/B/C/D/E. Uncontrolled = G.
  • A: 18,000 MSL through FL600, IFR generally required.
  • B: busiest airports, tailored wedding cake, explicit ATC clearance before entry.
  • C: typical 5-NM core / 10-NM shelf / 4,000 AAE, two-way communication before entry.
  • D: towered airport, typically surface to 2,500 AAE, two-way communication before entry.
  • E: controlled airspace not A-D; often starts 700 or 1,200 AGL.
  • G: uncontrolled; weather minima vary.
  • Never use generic shapes instead of the current sectional/Chart Supplement.
