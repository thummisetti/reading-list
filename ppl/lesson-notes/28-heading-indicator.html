Lesson 28 — The Heading Indicator

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The heading indicator (HI), also called a directional gyro (DG), gives a stable, easy-to-read heading reference.
  • A conventional HI is a gyroscopic instrument: the spinning gyro tends to maintain its orientation while the airplane rotates around it.
  • Unlike the magnetic compass, the display is arranged intuitively: the miniature airplane sits inside the compass card, so headings move in the direction expected during a turn.

Why use it if a magnetic compass is required?

  • The lesson first contrasts the HI with the magnetic compass, whose card can appear to move “backwards” and is subject to dip, acceleration, and turning errors.
  • The HI is therefore the primary convenient heading reference in normal flight, while the magnetic compass supplies the magnetic reference used to set/check it.
  • A conventional HI itself is not the VFR-day “magnetic direction indicator” required by the regulation; the magnetic compass fills that role.

Gyroscopic principles

  • Rigidity in space: a rapidly spinning gyro resists changes to the orientation of its spin axis.
  • The airplane and instrument case turn around the stabilized gyro; the mechanism converts that relative motion into the heading display.
  • Traditional training-aircraft HIs are often vacuum-driven, although electric units exist. Modern electronic displays may derive heading from AHRS/magnetometer systems instead of a mechanical gyro.

Setting and reading the HI

  • Use the setting knob to rotate the compass card until the HI agrees with the magnetic compass.
  • Make the comparison in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight when possible because that is when the magnetic compass is least affected by acceleration/turning error.
  • Common training practice is to check alignment approximately every 15 minutes and after maneuvering as needed.
  • Read the heading under the fixed lubber/reference line; the numbers are commonly abbreviated by tens (e.g., 27 = 270°).

Drift / precession

  • Mechanical friction, imperfect bearings, gyro precession, and Earth-related effects cause a free gyro heading indication to drift over time.
  • The lesson's practical takeaway is simple: a conventional unslaved HI must be periodically reset to the magnetic compass.
  • Slaved/electronic heading systems can automatically correct toward a magnetometer reference, but they have their own sensors and failure annunciations.

Failure clues

  • Unexpected drift, a frozen card, sluggish response, an OFF/failure flag, or disagreement with the magnetic compass/GPS track/other heading sources can indicate trouble.
  • Know the HI's power source. In many legacy panels a vacuum failure can affect both heading and attitude gyros, while an electrically powered turn coordinator may remain available.
  • A heading source can fail convincingly, so cross-check rather than fixating on one indication.

Heading is not track

  • The HI shows where the airplane's longitudinal axis is pointed—heading.
  • Wind can make the airplane's path over the ground—track—different from heading. Do not “correct” a healthy HI simply because GPS track differs.

What to remember

  • HI/DG = stable gyroscopic heading reference.
  • Set/check a conventional HI against the magnetic compass in straight, unaccelerated flight.
  • Expect drift; approximately 15-minute checks are a common training habit.
  • Know whether your system is vacuum, electric, or AHRS/magnetometer-based.
  • Heading ≠ ground track.
