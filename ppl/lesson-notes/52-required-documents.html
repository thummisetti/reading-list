Lesson 52 — Required Documents

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Legal flight depends on both the pilot being properly certificated/qualified and the aircraft having the required certificates, limitations, inspections, and equipment.
  • The instructor emphasizes looking up the actual FAR references and even bookmarking frequently used rules for checkride preparation rather than relying only on memory aids.

Pilot documents — what must be accessible

  • Under current §61.3, a required pilot flight crewmember must have the appropriate pilot certificate or acceptable temporary authority in physical possession or readily accessible in the aircraft.
  • The pilot must also have an acceptable photo identification in physical possession or readily accessible.
  • When a medical certificate is required, the appropriate medical certificate or other FAA-acceptable medical qualification must likewise be possessed/readily accessible. BasicMed is an alternative qualification only when its conditions are met.
  • On request, required certificates/medical qualification and photo ID must be presented to authorized FAA/NTSB/law-enforcement/TSA representatives as specified in §61.3.

Qualifications beyond the cards

  • Having the physical documents does not by itself make a flight legal. The pilot must also hold the required category/class/type and instrument privileges where applicable.
  • Flight review, endorsements, passenger-carrying recent experience, and any operation-specific requirements still apply.
  • The lesson’s larger point is useful for oral exams: be able to distinguish what document you carry from what qualification/currency you must have.

Aircraft certificates — §91.203

  • A civil aircraft generally must contain an appropriate and current airworthiness certificate and an effective registration certificate (or an allowed substitute under the regulation).
  • The airworthiness certificate must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so it is legible to passengers or crew.
  • Registration belongs to the specific aircraft; verify that it is current and matches the airplane.

Operating limitations — §91.9

  • The aircraft must be operated in accordance with its approved operating limitations.
  • Depending on certification/aircraft, those limitations may be found in an approved AFM/POH, placards, markings, supplements, or combinations of those sources.
  • Required markings and placards are not decorative—they are part of the operating-limitations system.

ARROW memory aid

  • A — Airworthiness certificate.
  • R — Registration certificate.
  • R — Radio station license: generally not a domestic FAA aircraft-document requirement, but FCC/international requirements can apply when operating internationally.
  • O — Operating limitations, including the approved manual/placards/markings as applicable.
  • W — Weight-and-balance information/current loading data for the specific airplane.

Important ARROW caveat

  • ARROW is a study mnemonic, not the wording of one single regulation. §91.203 expressly addresses airworthiness and registration; §91.9 addresses operating limitations. Weight-and-balance information comes from the aircraft’s approved data and airworthiness/operating-limitations framework.
  • Do not answer a checkride question by quoting the mnemonic alone—be ready to identify where the actual requirement comes from.

Airworthiness is more than paperwork

  • A certificate can be present while the airplane is still not airworthy for the flight.
  • Required inspections, airworthiness directives, maintenance status, equipment required by the type design/KOEL/§91.205 or the operation, and inoperative-equipment rules must also be considered.
  • The owner/operator has continuing maintenance responsibilities; the PIC must determine the aircraft is in condition for safe flight before operating it.

What to remember

  • Pilot: appropriate certificate/authority + acceptable photo ID + applicable medical qualification, plus the necessary ratings/currency/endorsements.
  • Aircraft: current airworthiness and registration, approved operating limitations, current loading information, and legal/airworthy inspection/equipment status.
  • ARROW is useful, but the FAR references are the authority.
