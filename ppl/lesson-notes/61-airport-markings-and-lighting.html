Lesson 61 — Airport Markings & Lighting

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The lesson frames airport signs, markings, and lighting as a runway-incursion prevention system: they tell you where you are, where you are going, and where you must stop.
  • The most useful color rule is simple: runway surface markings are white; taxiway markings are yellow.
  • Do not continue taxiing when unsure of location or clearance. Stop in a safe place and ask ATC or consult the airport diagram rather than guessing.

Runway designators

  • Runway numbers approximate the magnetic direction of the runway to the nearest 10 degrees with the final zero omitted: roughly 180° becomes Runway 18.
  • Reciprocal runway ends differ by 18 in designation because they point approximately 180° apart.
  • Parallel runways add L, C, or R; airports with more parallels may use shifted runway numbers as specified by airport-design standards.

Runway markings

  • Runway centerlines, threshold bars/stripes, runway numbers, aiming-point blocks, touchdown-zone markings, and side stripes are white.
  • Not every runway has every marking; markings become more extensive with runway approach type and operational requirements.
  • Aiming-point markings are large rectangular blocks used as a visual touchdown reference; touchdown-zone markings provide additional distance/position cues on equipped runways.

Displaced thresholds and chevrons

  • A displaced threshold uses white arrows leading to an arrowhead/threshold bar. The pavement before the displaced threshold is not available for landing touchdown from that direction.
  • Depending on published conditions, the displaced area may be usable for taxi, takeoff, or landing rollout from the opposite direction.
  • Yellow chevrons identify blast-pad/stopway or other pavement not intended for normal taxi, takeoff, or landing operations. Do not confuse chevrons with displaced-threshold arrows.

Taxiway markings

  • Taxiway centerlines are continuous yellow lines. Edge markings define taxiway edges and may be continuous or dashed depending on whether adjacent pavement is usable by aircraft.
  • Enhanced taxiway centerline markings add dashed yellow lines on each side of the centerline as the taxiway approaches a runway holding position. The transcript notes they begin roughly 150 ft before the hold marking and warn the pilot to prepare to stop.
  • Surface-painted location/direction markings may supplement signs at complex airports.
  • Roadway markings identify routes intended for ground vehicles; recognize them so an aircraft does not accidentally follow a vehicle lane.

Runway holding-position marking

  • The standard runway holding marking is two solid and two dashed yellow lines.
  • Approaching a runway from the taxiway side, the solid pair is on your side: stop before crossing the solid lines unless authorized to enter or cross.
  • When exiting a runway, continue past the entire holding-position marking; the aircraft is not clear until all parts are beyond it.
  • At a towered airport, read back runway hold-short instructions with the runway identifier as required and do not infer permission to cross a runway from an unrelated taxi instruction.

ILS critical-area marking

  • The ILS critical-area holding-position marking is a distinct yellow ladder-like pattern.
  • ATC may instruct aircraft to hold short of it when necessary to protect ILS signal integrity, especially in low ceilings or visibility.
  • It is separate from the runway holding-position marking; know which line an instruction refers to.

Airport signs

  • Red background / white characters: mandatory instruction, including runway holding-position, ILS critical-area, and no-entry signs.
  • Black background / yellow characters: location sign—tells you where you are.
  • Yellow background / black characters: direction, destination, or other routing/information sign.
  • Direction signs normally include arrows; location signs do not. Use signs together with the airport diagram to confirm orientation.

Runway lighting

  • Runway edge lights are generally white. On certain instrument runways, the final portion may show yellow to alert the pilot that the runway end is approaching.
  • Threshold/end lights are directional: green identifies the landing threshold from the approach side; red identifies the runway end from the runway side.
  • REILs are synchronized flashing lights near the runway threshold that make the runway end easier to identify.
  • Runway centerline and touchdown-zone lighting may be installed at larger or precision-equipped airports and uses standardized color transitions near the runway end.

Taxiway lighting

  • Blue lights mark taxiway edges.
  • Green lights mark taxiway centerlines where installed.
  • Runway guard lights—flashing or elevated yellow fixtures near a runway holding position—provide another warning that the pilot is approaching a runway environment.

Visual glidepath systems

  • PAPI and VASI systems provide visual vertical-path guidance. The exact display differs, but the core red/white logic is similar: more white means high; more red means low.
  • For a typical four-light PAPI: two white/two red = on glidepath, more white = high, more red = low, all red = well below.
  • Visual glidepath guidance does not replace obstacle awareness, displaced-threshold information, NOTAMs, or aircraft performance planning.

Pilot-controlled lighting

  • At some airports, pilots activate or change lighting intensity by keying the microphone on a published frequency within a specified period.
  • Use the Chart Supplement/current airport data for the correct frequency and activation sequence; do not assume every airport uses the same setup.

Runway-incursion habits

  • Brief the airport diagram before taxi, especially at unfamiliar airports or at night.
  • Keep the diagram accessible, follow the centerline, verify signs against the taxi route, and minimize distractions near runways.
  • If a routing instruction, sign, or marking does not make sense, stop and clarify.

What to remember

  • Runway markings white; taxiway markings yellow.
  • Red sign = mandatory; black = location; yellow = direction/destination/information.
  • Hold short before the solid pair when approaching a runway.
  • Enhanced taxiway centerline = runway approaching.
  • Blue taxiway edges; green taxiway centerline.
  • Typical PAPI: 2 white / 2 red = on path.
  • Stop and ask rather than improvise when surface position is uncertain.
