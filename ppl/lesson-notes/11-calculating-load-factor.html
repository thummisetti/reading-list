Lesson 11 — Calculating Load Factor

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
This lesson turns the previous concepts into exam-style calculations: determine load factor from bank angle, then use that load factor to estimate accelerated stall speed.

Core relationships

  • For an ideal coordinated, constant-altitude turn, load factor depends on bank angle: n = 1 / cos(bank angle).
  • As bank increases, load factor rises slowly at first and very rapidly beyond about 60°.
  • Accelerated stall speed follows VS,n = VS,1G × √n.
  • Therefore: more bank → more G (if altitude is held) → higher stall speed.

Load-factor checkpoints worth knowing

  • 0° bank: 1.00 G.
  • 30° bank: about 1.15 G.
  • 45° bank: about 1.41 G.
  • 60° bank: exactly 2.00 G in the ideal level-turn relationship.
  • 75° bank: about 3.86 G.
  • As bank approaches 90°, cos(bank) approaches zero, so the theoretical load factor needed to hold altitude approaches infinity.
  • The practical exam memory points are especially 45° ≈ 1.4 G and 60° = 2 G.

Using the FAA testing-supplement chart

  • The instructor emphasizes that you may not need to derive every answer from trigonometry on the written exam because the testing supplement contains a load-factor-versus-bank-angle graph.
  • Use the bank angle on the horizontal axis, move to the load-factor curve, then read the corresponding G value.
  • The graph makes the nonlinearity obvious: the curve stays relatively flat at shallow/moderate bank and turns sharply upward at steep bank.
  • Know how to read the graph even if you also know the formula; exam questions may be built around the supplied figure.

Calculating accelerated stall speed

  • Once you know load factor, take the square root of n and multiply it by the applicable 1-G stall speed.
  • At 2 G: √2 ≈ 1.414, so stall speed rises about 41%.
  • At 4 G: √4 = 2, so stall speed doubles.
  • The transcript works an example using a roughly 40-kt original stall speed and a load-factor multiplier rounded to about 1.42, producing a new stall speed around 56.8 kt.
  • Small differences in a worked answer can come from rounding the load factor or square root; carry enough precision until the final step.

Why the calculation matters operationally

  • The instructor gives a low-altitude example: climbing around 60 kt to clear an obstacle, then abruptly banking to about 60° to avoid something.
  • If the pilot tries to hold altitude/climb path in that steep bank, the load factor rises toward 2 G and the stall speed can move much closer to the current indicated airspeed.
  • This is why a speed that seems comfortably above the 1-G stall speed can become dangerously close to stall in a steep, loaded maneuver.
  • The danger is greatest when low altitude leaves little recovery margin.

Assumptions and limits of the bank-angle formula

  • n = 1/cos(bank) assumes a coordinated turn with altitude held constant.
  • If the airplane is allowed to descend, climbs, slips/skids, encounters turbulence, or has vertical acceleration, actual load factor can differ.
  • Bank angle by itself does not force a particular G unless the pilot is also demanding the corresponding curved/level flight path.
  • Use actual aircraft POH/AFM data and limitations in real operations.

Calculation workflow

  • 1. Identify bank angle and confirm the problem assumes a coordinated level turn.
  • 2. Find load factor from the FAA chart or calculate 1/cos(bank).
  • 3. If asked for accelerated stall speed, calculate √load factor.
  • 4. Multiply the applicable 1-G stall speed by that square-root factor.
  • 5. Sanity-check: steeper bank should never produce a lower level-turn load factor or lower accelerated stall speed.

Worth watching — testing-supplement graph

  • The visual graph is the most useful part of the lesson to see once because it shows how dramatically load factor rises after 60° bank.
  • The worked stall-speed example is also useful if square roots/load-factor multipliers are not intuitive.

What to remember for review

  • Level coordinated turn: n = 1/cos(bank).
  • 30° ≈ 1.15 G; 45° ≈ 1.41 G; 60° = 2 G; 75° ≈ 3.86 G.
  • Accelerated stall: VSn = VS1G × √n.
  • 2 G → stall speed × 1.414; 4 G → stall speed × 2.
  • Use the supplied FAA chart when the exam provides it.
  • The relationships assume a coordinated, level turn; descending or other acceleration changes the result.
  • High bank at low airspeed/altitude can erase stall margin rapidly.
