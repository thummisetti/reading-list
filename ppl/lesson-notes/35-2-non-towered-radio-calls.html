Lesson 35.2 — Non-Towered Radio Calls

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • At a non-towered airport, pilots use CTAF self-announcements to make their position and intentions predictable to other pilots.
  • The radio does not control traffic and does not create right-of-way; see-and-avoid, right-of-way rules, and predictable pattern behavior remain primary.
  • The transcript emphasizes being clear, concise, and useful rather than filling the frequency with unnecessary commentary.

Finding the frequency

  • Find CTAF on the sectional, Chart Supplement, or current airport data.
  • The CTAF may be a UNICOM frequency, MULTICOM frequency, or a tower frequency used for advisory traffic calls when the tower is closed.
  • Obtain AWOS/ASOS/other weather first when available so you can choose an appropriate runway/pattern and avoid using CTAF for information already available elsewhere.

Basic self-announce structure

  • A strong template is: airport name → aircraft type/callsign → position → intentions → airport name.
  • Putting the airport name at both beginning and end helps listeners sharing the frequency know which field the call concerns.
  • Use plain, standard traffic-pattern terms: “left downwind runway 17,” “left base runway 17,” “final runway 17,” etc.

Inbound sequence

  • Monitor CTAF early enough to build a picture of active traffic before entering the pattern.
  • Make an inbound/advisory call with position, altitude when useful, and intended runway/pattern entry.
  • Enter the standard/published pattern in a predictable way and avoid maneuvering that cuts in front of established traffic.
  • Common useful calls include entering downwind, base, final, and clear of the runway.

Departure sequence

  • Before taxi/takeoff, monitor traffic and make calls that help others understand runway use and intentions.
  • Announce taking the runway/departing and, where useful, whether you will remain in the pattern or depart in a particular direction.
  • After landing, do not announce “clear” until the entire aircraft is actually beyond the runway holding-position markings and no part remains on the runway side.

Position wording

  • Use positions other pilots can quickly visualize: miles/direction from the field, pattern leg, runway number, and altitude when relevant.
  • Do not use vague relative wording that only makes sense from your cockpit.
  • At airports with similar names or on congested shared frequencies, clear airport identification is especially important.

Pattern information

  • Most traffic patterns use left turns unless right traffic is published/indicated.
  • Determine runway selection from wind, runway conditions, published restrictions, existing traffic flow, and operational considerations—not simply because one pilot announced a runway.
  • Do not force your preferred runway into an established conflicting flow unless safety requires it; communicate and sequence appropriately.

NORDO / silent traffic

  • Not every legal aircraft has a radio, and a pilot can have a radio failure. Silence on CTAF never means the airport is empty.
  • Scan for aircraft on the ground, in all pattern legs, straight-in, opposite-direction, and crossing the runway.
  • A radio call supplements your eyes; it does not replace them.

Recommended procedures vs. legal right-of-way

  • The lesson describes recommended self-announcement practices. Good practice dramatically improves predictability, but a missed or imperfect radio call does not automatically decide who has legal right-of-way.
  • Likewise, saying “final” or “taking the runway” does not reserve airspace/runway. Continue to look, yield as required, and avoid collision.

What to remember

  • CTAF = coordination/self-announcement, not control.
  • Airport → callsign → position → intentions → airport.
  • Monitor early; make useful downwind/base/final/clear calls.
  • Use published traffic direction.
  • Expect NORDO traffic and keep scanning.
  • Calls do not create right-of-way.
