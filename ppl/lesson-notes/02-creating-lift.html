Lesson 2 — Creating Lift

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
These notes are intended to contain the lesson's substantive teaching points without requiring a rewatch. Repetition and conversational filler are removed; demonstrations and visual explanations are called out separately.

Core idea

  • Lift is the aerodynamic force that opposes weight and supports the airplane.
  • In steady, unaccelerated flight, the lesson reviews that lift equals weight.
  • The wing produces aerodynamic force through its interaction with relative airflow. The lesson explains lift using both pressure differences associated with Bernoulli's principle and Newton's third law / downward turning of airflow.
  • The pilot can change lift by changing airspeed, wing shape/camber, or angle of attack.
  • Increasing lift is not free: changes such as greater camber or greater AOA also increase drag, so airspeed and flight path change unless power/pitch are adjusted.

Wing and airfoil

  • The lesson treats the wings as the airplane's primary lift-producing surfaces.
  • An airfoil is the cross-sectional shape of a wing (or another surface designed to produce an aerodynamic reaction).
  • The shape of the airfoil, the airflow over it, and its orientation to that airflow determine the aerodynamic forces it produces.
  • The lesson begins from two broad ingredients introduced in Lesson 1: relative wind + wing shape.

Bernoulli's principle and pressure

  • The instructor says the written exam is especially likely to expect familiarity with Bernoulli's principle.
  • For the simplified relationship used in the lesson: as the velocity of a moving fluid increases, its static pressure decreases.
  • A Venturi is used as an analogy: constricting a flowing fluid makes it speed up and is associated with lower static pressure in the constricted region.
  • Airflow around a lifting wing has a non-uniform velocity and pressure distribution. In the lesson's explanation, air moves faster over the upper surface, giving lower pressure above the wing than the pressure acting below it.
  • The resulting pressure distribution produces a net upward aerodynamic force.
  • The useful concept is therefore: velocity distribution ↔ pressure distribution ↔ aerodynamic force.

Accuracy note — don't memorize the “equal transit time” story

  • The video says air traveling over the more-curved upper surface has farther to go and therefore must travel faster so it can meet the lower-surface air at the trailing edge.
  • That “longer path / equal transit time” explanation is a common simplification, but it is not a physical requirement. Air parcels that separate at the leading edge do not have to reunite at the trailing edge at the same time.
  • Do keep the valid pieces: a lifting wing generally has different velocities and pressures around its surfaces, Bernoulli's relationship can connect local velocity and pressure, and the integrated pressure distribution contributes to lift.
  • Likewise, a wing is not literally half of a Venturi nozzle. Use the Venturi only as an intuition for the speed/pressure relationship.

Relative wind / airflow seen by the wing

  • Relative wind is conventionally defined as airflow parallel to and opposite the aircraft's flight path.
  • The lesson informally discusses the local airflow over the airplane as being influenced by several things: the aircraft's motion through the air, propeller slipstream/“prop wash,” and the ambient atmospheric wind.
  • Prop wash is the rearward accelerated airflow produced by the propeller. The instructor compares it to air from a fan.
  • Increasing thrust can accelerate the airplane and also changes propeller slipstream; as airspeed through the air increases, aerodynamic forces increase.
  • A headwind gives greater airspeed for a given groundspeed; a tailwind gives less airspeed for a given groundspeed.
  • This is one reason airplanes normally take off and land into the wind: the required aerodynamic airspeed is reached at a lower groundspeed, improving takeoff/landing performance.

Camber and flaps

  • Camber describes the curvature of an airfoil.
  • Increasing camber changes the pressure/flow pattern so the wing can produce more lift at a given condition.
  • The pilot can increase camber by extending flaps.
  • The lesson describes flap extension as changing/lengthening the wing's effective curved surface.
  • More camber → more lift capability, but also more drag.
  • Because drag rises when the flaps are extended, the airplane tends to slow unless power/pitch are adjusted.
  • Before extending the flaps in the flight demonstration, the instructor checks that the airspeed is in the white arc, the flap operating range. In actual operation, obey the airplane's POH/AFM and flap-extension speed limits.

Angle of attack

  • A third way to increase lift is to increase angle of attack (AOA), up to the wing's critical AOA.
  • AOA is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative wind; it is not simply the airplane's pitch angle.
  • In the lesson's flight condition, raising the nose increases AOA, which increases lift demand and induced drag.
  • As AOA increases in the demonstration without enough additional power to maintain speed, airspeed bleeds off.
  • The lesson intentionally defers the detailed AOA/stall explanation to Lesson 3.

Newton's third law and flow turning

  • Newton's third law: for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction.
  • The lesson illustrates this by showing relative airflow being deflected and the wing receiving an opposite aerodynamic reaction.
  • A complete picture involves both upper and lower wing surfaces turning the airflow, not just air molecules “hitting the bottom” of the wing.
  • Bernoulli and Newton are not competing explanations: the pressure field around the wing and the net turning/change in momentum of the airflow are two compatible ways of describing the same aerodynamic interaction.
  • As AOA increases (before the stall), the flow is turned more strongly and lift coefficient generally increases; drag also increases.

Three practical ways the lesson increases lift

  • 1. Increase airspeed: greater velocity through the air increases aerodynamic force; lift varies strongly with airspeed for a given configuration/AOA.
  • 2. Increase camber: extend flaps/change the airfoil shape, increasing lift capability and drag.
  • 3. Increase AOA: change the wing's orientation to the relative wind, increasing lift coefficient until critical AOA is reached; drag rises too.
  • The practical lesson is not merely to memorize three items. Each method changes the airplane's energy, drag, airspeed, and/or flight path, so control inputs interact.

Flight demonstration 1 — increase power / airspeed (about 5:20)

  • The airplane begins trimmed for approximately level, unaccelerated flight.
  • The instructor deliberately does not pull back on the yoke; he increases power and lets airspeed increase.
  • He uses rudder to keep the airplane pointed straight as power is increased.
  • As airspeed and lift increase in the demonstrated trimmed condition, the airplane begins to climb.
  • The demonstration connects the chain: more power → acceleration / more airflow → changed lift balance → climb.

Worth watching: the hands-off/trimmed response makes the connection between power, speed, lift, and flight path intuitive.

Flight demonstration 2 — extend flaps / increase camber (about 6:03)

  • The instructor first verifies the airplane is within the white arc before lowering the flaps.
  • Extending the flaps increases camber and the airplane initially tends to climb as lift changes.
  • The instructor points out the other side of the tradeoff: the added drag causes airspeed to decrease.
  • He later retracts the flaps after establishing adequate airspeed.
  • The visual takeaway is flaps change both lift and drag; they are not simply a “more lift” switch.

Worth watching: useful for seeing the immediate pitch/flight-path/airspeed reaction to flap extension.

Flight demonstration 3 — increase AOA (about 6:42)

  • With the airplane around 2,600 ft in the demonstration, the instructor pitches up to increase AOA.
  • The increased AOA increases lift coefficient but also increases induced drag.
  • Airspeed visibly bleeds off as the nose is held higher.
  • This sets up the next lesson: continuing to increase AOA eventually reaches the critical AOA and causes a stall.

Worth watching if the distinction between “more lift coefficient” and “the airplane must keep climbing” is not yet intuitive.

What to remember for review

  • Lift is an aerodynamic force produced by the wing/airflow interaction and, in steady unaccelerated flight, balances weight.
  • Bernoulli: higher local flow velocity is associated with lower static pressure under the conditions used in the lesson.
  • Do not rely on the equal-transit-time idea that upper and lower air parcels must meet again at the trailing edge.
  • Newton: the wing turns airflow; the airflow exerts an equal-and-opposite force on the wing.
  • Relative wind is opposite the flight path; ambient wind changes groundspeed for a given airspeed.
  • Increasing camber with flaps increases lift capability and drag.
  • Increasing AOA increases lift coefficient until critical AOA; drag increases as well.
  • Three ways emphasized by the lesson: increase airspeed, increase camber, increase AOA.
  • Always think in coupled effects: a change that produces more lift may also produce more drag, less airspeed, or a changed flight path.
