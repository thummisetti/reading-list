Lesson 6 — Airplane Stability

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Stability is not just a written-test vocabulary topic. The instructor's main practical point is that a properly loaded, properly trimmed training airplane is designed to help return toward its equilibrium instead of requiring the pilot to fight every disturbance.

Core idea

  • Stability is an airplane's tendency after it is disturbed from equilibrium: does it initially move back toward the original condition, stay displaced, or move farther away?
  • Stability reduces pilot workload because a stable airplane tends to oppose disturbances such as turbulence.
  • Two different ideas must be kept separate: static stability describes the initial response; dynamic stability describes what happens over time.
  • Stability exists about all three axes: pitch/longitudinal stability, roll/lateral stability, and yaw/directional stability.
  • Aircraft geometry, tail surfaces, wing placement/dihedral, and CG location all influence stability.

Equilibrium and disturbance

  • An airplane in equilibrium is in a condition where the relevant forces and moments are balanced for its current flight state.
  • A gust, turbulence, control input, configuration change, or power change can disturb that balance.
  • The instructor's simple example: turbulence pitches the nose up. A stable airplane has aerodynamic tendencies that drive it back toward its original trimmed condition rather than continuing to pitch away.
  • A stable airplane may overshoot the original condition and oscillate before settling; that is where dynamic stability becomes important.

Static stability — the initial tendency

  • Positive static stability: immediately after a disturbance, the airplane tends back toward its original equilibrium.
  • Neutral static stability: after being displaced, it initially tends to remain at the new displacement rather than returning or diverging.
  • Negative static stability: the initial tendency is to continue moving farther away from the original equilibrium.
  • Typical training airplanes are deliberately designed with positive static stability because this makes them more forgiving and lowers workload.
  • The transcript uses fighter aircraft as an example of designs that may trade natural stability for maneuverability and rely heavily on control systems. Treat the broad point as a stability-versus-maneuverability tradeoff rather than assuming every fighter is literally statically unstable in every axis/configuration.

Dynamic stability — what happens over time

  • Dynamic stability assumes the airplane has already been disturbed and asks how the resulting motion evolves.
  • Positive dynamic stability: the oscillations decrease in amplitude until the airplane returns to equilibrium.
  • Neutral dynamic stability: the airplane keeps oscillating with approximately constant amplitude.
  • Negative dynamic stability: the oscillations grow larger with time.
  • An airplane can have positive static stability yet poor dynamic behavior; therefore “static” and “dynamic” are not interchangeable.
  • The desirable training-airplane behavior is generally a restoring initial tendency plus oscillations that damp out.

Longitudinal stability — pitch

  • Longitudinal stability refers to pitch stability about the lateral axis.
  • In a conventional airplane, the CG is typically ahead of the wing's effective lift/pressure resultant, producing a nose-down pitching tendency that the horizontal tail balances with an aerodynamic force/moment.
  • If a disturbance pitches the nose down, the airplane tends to accelerate. Greater airflow over the tail changes tail force and can create a restoring nose-up moment.
  • If the nose pitches up and airspeed decays, the tail force changes in the opposite direction and the nose tends to return downward.
  • The result is the familiar phugoid-like exchange between pitch, airspeed, and altitude: the airplane can oscillate above/below the trimmed condition while the oscillations gradually damp.

CG location and longitudinal stability

  • Forward CG generally increases longitudinal stability because a larger restoring tail moment is required to balance the airplane.
  • The tradeoff is greater tail downforce/control force and potentially reduced performance or nose-up authority in some conditions.
  • Aft CG reduces longitudinal stability; less restoring moment exists and the airplane is easier to pitch away from equilibrium.
  • An excessively aft CG can reduce stall/spin recovery effectiveness because it can be harder to produce the required nose-down pitching moment.
  • Loading outside the approved CG envelope is therefore not merely a paperwork error; it changes the airplane's stability and controllability.

Trim and the airplane's preferred condition

  • The lesson gives strong practical emphasis to trim: when airspeed changes, trim should be adjusted so the airplane can maintain the desired condition without sustained control force.
  • A properly trimmed stable airplane tends to return toward the trimmed airspeed/condition after a small pitch disturbance.
  • Example from the transcript: push the nose down and the airplane speeds up; the increased aerodynamic tail force creates a restoring pitch-up tendency. As it pitches back up, it slows, overshoots, and repeats with smaller oscillations.
  • The instructor's practical takeaway is that once a stable airplane is balanced and trimmed, it can “almost fly itself” in the sense that the pilot need not continuously hold heavy pressure just to keep a normal condition.
  • Trim does not replace correct pitch/power control. First establish the desired attitude/airspeed, then trim off the control pressure.

Lateral stability — roll

  • Lateral stability refers to roll stability about the longitudinal axis.
  • Dihedral — wings angled upward from root to tip when viewed from the front — is a major design feature that promotes roll stability.
  • When the airplane is disturbed into a sideslip, the wing geometry causes the lower/upwind wing to experience a different effective AOA/airflow and generate a restoring lift difference.
  • This rolling tendency helps return the wings toward level.
  • The instructor connects this back to dynamic stability: the airplane may roll past level and oscillate, but in a positively dynamically stable design those oscillations become smaller.
  • Sweepback can also contribute to lateral stability because a sideslip changes the effective sweep/airflow seen by each wing and therefore their relative lift.

High-wing / keel or pendulum effect

  • The lesson discusses the stabilizing tendency associated with a high-wing airplane, calling it a “keel effect”/pendulum-like effect.
  • The simplified picture is that the airplane's mass/CG hangs below the wing's principal lift region, which can contribute to a restoring roll tendency when displaced.
  • In real aerodynamics, high-wing lateral stability also involves wing-fuselage interference and sideslip effects; do not reduce all high-wing stability to gravity acting like a literal hanging pendulum.
  • The practical takeaway remains: wing placement is one of several design choices that influence lateral stability.

Directional stability — yaw

  • Directional stability is stability about the vertical/yaw axis.
  • The vertical stabilizer and the airplane's side area behind the CG act like a weather vane.
  • If a yaw disturbance produces a sideslip, airflow strikes the tail from the side and creates a restoring yawing moment that tends to realign the nose with the relative wind.
  • More effective vertical-tail/side area aft of the CG generally increases directional stability; side area ahead of the CG tends to work against it.
  • Directional stability helps the airplane resist unwanted yaw but does not eliminate the pilot's need to use rudder for coordination.

Stability versus controllability

  • Greater stability is usually helpful in a trainer because it reduces workload and resists accidental departures from the desired condition.
  • But very high stability also means the airplane strongly resists being displaced, potentially requiring larger control forces.
  • Highly maneuverable aircraft may accept less natural stability so they can change attitude rapidly; the next lesson focuses on controllability, which is the ability to deliberately move the airplane where the pilot wants it.

Worth watching — static vs dynamic diagrams

  • The lesson's graphs/animations are useful for distinguishing the six combinations: positive/neutral/negative static tendency and positive/neutral/negative dynamic oscillation behavior.
  • The pitch-disturbance animation makes the difference especially clear: static stability asks which way the airplane first moves; dynamic stability asks whether subsequent oscillations shrink, stay constant, or grow.

Worth watching — dihedral and high-wing examples

  • The wing-front-view diagrams show how dihedral creates a restoring roll response in sideslip.
  • The high-wing/pendulum analogy is easier to understand visually, even though the underlying aerodynamics are more nuanced than a literal weight hanging under a pivot.

What to remember for review

  • Static stability = initial tendency.
  • Dynamic stability = behavior over time.
  • Positive static: toward equilibrium; neutral: stay displaced; negative: away.
  • Positive dynamic: oscillations shrink; neutral: stay same size; negative: grow.
  • Longitudinal stability = pitch; conventional tail + CG relationship creates restoring moments.
  • Forward CG generally more stable; aft CG less stable.
  • Lateral stability = roll; dihedral, sweep, wing placement, and sideslip effects contribute.
  • Directional stability = yaw; vertical tail acts like a weather vane.
  • A correctly loaded, correctly trimmed stable airplane tends to return toward its trimmed condition after a disturbance.
  • Trim reduces sustained control pressure; it does not substitute for flying the airplane.
