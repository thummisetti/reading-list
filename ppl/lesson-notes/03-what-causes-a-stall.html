Lesson 3 — What Causes a Stall?

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Use this as a low-bandwidth replacement for a rewatch. It keeps the instructional points and removes repetition, ads, and filler.

Core idea

  • A stall is a rapid loss of lift caused by exceeding the wing's critical angle of attack.
  • A stall is fundamentally an AOA problem, not an airspeed problem. Stall airspeed changes with weight, load factor, configuration, and maneuvering.
  • The universal first recovery action is to reduce angle of attack.

Airfoil reference lines

  • The front of the airfoil is the leading edge; the rear is the trailing edge.
  • The chord line is an imaginary straight line from leading edge to trailing edge.
  • AOA is measured between this chord line and relative wind.

Relative wind and AOA

  • Relative wind acts opposite the airplane's flight path and changes direction as the flight path changes.
  • Angle of attack is the angle between relative wind and the wing's chord line.
  • Increasing AOA increases lift only up to a limit; beyond that limit the airflow can no longer remain adequately attached.

Boundary layer and separation

  • At lower AOA, airflow over the wing is comparatively smooth and organized in thin layers.
  • As AOA becomes excessive, airflow separates from the upper surface: boundary-layer separation.
  • Separated turbulent flow produces much less useful lift and more drag.
  • The wing still produces some lift after the stall; stall does not mean lift instantly becomes zero.

Critical angle of attack

  • Exceeding the critical AOA causes the wing to stall every time.
  • Altitude and weight can change the indicated airspeed at which critical AOA is reached, but not the defining stall mechanism.
  • An airplane can therefore stall nose-high, nose-low, slow, or relatively fast if the critical AOA is exceeded.
  • The lesson's single most important takeaway: exceed critical AOA → stall.

Recovery principle

  • Relax back pressure / lower the nose enough to get below critical AOA.
  • Reducing AOA allows airflow to reattach and restores normal lift capability.
  • Power, leveling the wings, configuration cleanup, and minimizing altitude loss matter, but none replaces breaking the stall by reducing AOA.
  • Avoid an abrupt pull that could cause a secondary stall.

Power-off stall demonstration / worth watching

  • The airplane is slowed and configured to simulate an approach/landing stall.
  • As the nose is raised, the instructor waits for stall indications such as buffeting and the stall break/wing drop.
  • At the break, back pressure is relaxed first, then power/configuration are managed and the airplane transitions back to a climb.
  • The exact flap/power sequence is aircraft- and procedure-specific; use the POH/AFM and your instructor. The constant principle is reduce AOA first.

What to remember

  • Chord line + relative wind define AOA.
  • Lift increases with AOA only until critical AOA.
  • Excessive AOA causes airflow separation and a rapid loss of lift.
  • Stall airspeed varies; critical AOA is the defining condition.
  • Recovery starts by reducing AOA.
