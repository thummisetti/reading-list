Lesson 3 — What Causes a Stall?

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
These notes preserve the lesson's substantive teaching points while removing the sponsor segment, repetition, and conversational filler. The demonstration procedure is included for understanding but is not a substitute for your aircraft's POH/AFM or your instructor's technique.

Core idea

  • A stall is a rapid reduction in lift caused by exceeding the wing's critical angle of attack (AOA).
  • The defining cause is AOA, not a particular airspeed. The airplane may reach critical AOA at different indicated airspeeds depending on weight, load factor, configuration, maneuvering, and other conditions.
  • The instructor repeatedly emphasizes the one point to remember above everything else: exceed critical AOA → the wing stalls.
  • The universal recovery principle is equally simple: reduce AOA below critical so attached airflow and normal lift capability can be restored.

Airfoil reference points and chord line

  • The lesson looks at the wing in cross-section as an airfoil.
  • The front of the airfoil is the leading edge; the rear is the trailing edge.
  • The chord line is an imaginary straight line connecting the leading edge and trailing edge.
  • The chord line gives us a fixed reference on the wing from which angle of attack can be measured.

Relative wind and angle of attack

  • Relative wind is airflow parallel to and opposite the airplane's flight path.
  • As the airplane's flight path changes, the direction of relative wind changes as well.
  • Angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative wind.
  • Pitch attitude and AOA are therefore not identical: AOA depends on both the airplane's attitude and the direction of its actual flight path.
  • The lesson informally revisits airflow sources such as aircraft motion and propeller slipstream. For the stall concept, the important geometric relationship is simply chord line versus relative wind.

Boundary layer and normal attached airflow

  • The instructor describes the airflow immediately adjacent to the wing as a boundary layer.
  • At a normal, non-stalled AOA, airflow over much of the wing remains comparatively smooth and attached to the surface.
  • The lesson describes orderly flow in thin layers as laminar flow.
  • As AOA increases, lift coefficient initially increases because the wing is interacting more strongly with the airflow.

What happens as AOA gets too high

  • As AOA continues to increase, the airflow has increasing difficulty following the upper surface of the wing.
  • The point at which airflow pulls away from the surface is called boundary-layer separation.
  • Behind the separation point, airflow becomes much more turbulent and disorganized rather than remaining smoothly attached.
  • As separation spreads, the wing's lift coefficient stops increasing and then falls sharply while drag rises.
  • A stalled wing still produces some lift; “stall” does not mean aerodynamic lift instantly becomes zero.
  • The practical consequence is that the wing can no longer produce enough lift in the existing condition to support the airplane as it did before the stall, so the airplane's flight path changes rapidly.

Critical angle of attack

  • The critical AOA is the AOA at which the wing reaches its maximum lift coefficient; increasing AOA beyond that point causes the stall.
  • Every wing/airfoil has a characteristic critical AOA for a given configuration and condition.
  • The instructor stresses that altitude and aircraft gross weight can change the airspeed at which the airplane reaches critical AOA, while the defining stall condition remains critical AOA.
  • Other factors such as load factor and configuration can also change the indicated stall speed.
  • An airplane can therefore stall at a variety of airspeeds and attitudes — slow or relatively fast, nose-high or even nose-low — if critical AOA is exceeded.
  • Written-test / conceptual takeaway: a stall is caused by exceeding critical AOA, not merely by “flying too slowly.”

Stall warning signs

  • The flight demonstration shows buffeting as an early tactile indication that separated/turbulent airflow is developing.
  • Depending on the airplane, the pilot may also have an aural stall warning or an AOA indication.
  • The demonstration then shows the actual stall break/drop after the buffet.
  • Do not wait for a dramatic break in real operations if the airplane is giving earlier stall cues; recognize the approach to a stall and correct it early.

Recovery principle

  • The most important recovery action is to reduce AOA.
  • In the lesson, this is done by relaxing aft yoke/back-stick pressure and allowing the nose to lower enough to get below critical AOA.
  • Reducing AOA allows airflow to reattach and restores the wing's ability to produce normal lift.
  • Then use appropriate power and configuration changes and establish the desired flight path while minimizing altitude loss.
  • Recover smoothly; an abrupt pull-up while still near critical AOA can cause a secondary stall.
  • The exact power, flap, carb-heat, and pitch sequence is aircraft- and procedure-specific. The invariant aerodynamic principle is break the stall by reducing AOA first.

Power-off stall demonstration — setup (about 6:46)

  • The instructor simulates getting too slow/high-AOA while approaching to land and demonstrates a power-off stall.
  • He says he wants the maneuver completed no lower than approximately 1,500 ft AGL and starts around 2,800 ft, expecting no more than roughly 500 ft of altitude loss. Treat those as the instructor's demonstration parameters, not universal operating rules.
  • He chooses and maintains a heading, verifies the airplane is in the white arc before extending full flaps, and runs configuration/check items.
  • For this airplane/demonstration he uses carb heat, idle power, full flaps, and pitches for roughly 60 kt to simulate the landing condition.
  • Once established at the simulated approach speed, he raises the nose toward the horizon and continues increasing AOA until the stall occurs.

Worth watching: useful for seeing how a normal-looking landing configuration is deliberately transitioned into a stall while the actual cause remains AOA.

Power-off stall demonstration — indications and recovery (about 7:50)

  • The instructor points out wing buffeting as the first stall indication in the demonstration.
  • He continues until the stall break/drop occurs.
  • At the break, the spoken sequence is essentially: maximum power, relax back pressure, carb heat off, retract the first increment of flaps, then manage pitch for recovery.
  • He explicitly states that relaxing the back pressure is the most important part because that is what reduces AOA and breaks the stall.
  • He then brings the nose toward the horizon, confirms a positive climb, and retracts the remaining flaps in stages as airspeed increases.
  • In his demonstration he waits for approximately 70 kt and a positive rate before completing flap retraction.
  • He notes that the staged flap-retraction details are his systematic procedure; the exact sequence should come from the aircraft POH/AFM and instructor guidance.

Worth watching: the buffet → break → relax → power/configuration sequence is much easier to internalize visually than from definitions alone.

Keep the aerodynamic hierarchy straight

  • Low airspeed does not itself define a stall. Low speed often leads a pilot to increase AOA to maintain lift, which is why stalls frequently occur at low indicated airspeeds.
  • Pitch attitude does not itself define a stall. AOA is measured against relative wind, not against the horizon.
  • The stall warning is not the stall. Buffet/horn/AOA indications warn that the wing is approaching or entering the stalled regime; critical AOA is the fundamental condition.
  • Recovery is not “add power and pull up.” Power can be very helpful, but the wing must first be brought below critical AOA.

What to remember for review

  • Chord line: leading edge to trailing edge.
  • Relative wind: opposite the flight path.
  • AOA: angle between chord line and relative wind.
  • Increasing AOA increases lift coefficient only up to the critical AOA.
  • Excessive AOA produces boundary-layer separation, loss of lift coefficient, and increased drag.
  • Critical AOA is the defining stall condition.
  • Stall speed varies with operating conditions; there is no single airspeed that defines all stalls.
  • Common stall cues include buffet, stall warning, and the stall break/drop.
  • Recovery starts by reducing AOA. Then use the aircraft-specific power/configuration recovery procedure.
