Lesson 50 — Weight and Balance

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • An airplane must be within both its approved weight limits and center-of-gravity envelope. Being below maximum gross weight does not guarantee acceptable balance.
  • Weight affects performance; CG location affects stability, control forces, stall behavior, and the ability to recover from unusual conditions.
  • The transcript uses a seesaw analogy: balance depends on both the amount of weight and its distance from the reference point.

Datum, arm, moment, CG

  • Datum: an imaginary reference plane chosen by the manufacturer.
  • Arm: horizontal distance from the datum to an item's center of gravity.
  • Moment: turning tendency created by a weight at a distance; moment = weight × arm.
  • Total aircraft CG arm = total moment ÷ total weight.
  • Some POHs divide moments by 100 or 1,000 for easier arithmetic; follow the chart/table convention exactly.

Use the actual airplane's data

  • Start with the current empty weight and empty-weight moment/CG for the specific aircraft, including installed equipment changes.
  • Add occupants, baggage, usable fuel, and other load items using the approved station arms or loading tables.
  • Do not substitute a generic model empty weight for the airplane's actual weight-and-balance record.

Loading calculation workflow

  1. List each item and its weight.
  2. Apply the approved arm or loading-table value.
  3. Compute/obtain each moment.
  4. Add all weights and all moments.
  5. Compute loaded CG or plot total weight/moment on the loading envelope, depending on the POH method.
  6. Verify both total weight and CG are inside limits.
  7. Consider how fuel burn changes both weight and CG during flight.

Why a forward CG matters

  • A conventional airplane normally uses tail-down force to balance the airplane about the CG.
  • Moving CG forward generally requires more tail-down force; the wing must then create more lift to support both aircraft weight and that additional downward tail load.
  • That usually means a higher required angle of attack for a given condition, more induced drag, reduced cruise/climb efficiency, greater control forces, and potentially a higher stall speed.
  • An excessively forward CG may leave insufficient elevator authority for rotation or landing flare.
  • Forward CG generally increases longitudinal stability, but “more stable” does not mean “within limits.”

Why an aft CG matters

  • Moving CG aft reduces the tail-down force required in normal flight, which can reduce trim drag and slightly improve performance.
  • The tradeoff is reduced longitudinal stability and smaller recovery/control margins.
  • Too-far-aft CG can make the airplane more sensitive in pitch and can make stall/spin recovery difficult because the tail may not have enough leverage/effectiveness to lower the nose as needed.
  • An aft CG may reduce stall speed somewhat, but that performance advantage is never justification for operating outside the approved envelope.

Weight effects

  • Higher weight requires more lift and therefore more angle of attack or airspeed.
  • Expect higher stall speed, longer takeoff roll, reduced climb, higher landing speed/energy, and longer landing distance as weight increases.
  • Weight limits and CG limits are separate checks; either one can make the loading unacceptable.

Fuel and changing CG

  • Fuel has weight and therefore contributes a moment. As fuel burns, total weight decreases and CG can move.
  • The direction and amount of CG movement depend on tank location relative to the datum/current CG.
  • For some airplanes it is useful or required to evaluate takeoff, zero-fuel, and landing conditions separately. Follow the POH/AFM method for the aircraft.

Accuracy note

  • The transcript's aerodynamic explanations are useful conceptual models, but exact performance and handling changes vary by aircraft design.
  • Use the approved POH/AFM and current aircraft weight-and-balance records as authority for limits and calculations.

What to remember

  • Moment = weight × arm.
  • CG = total moment ÷ total weight.
  • Check both weight and CG.
  • Forward CG: generally more stable, more tail load/drag, heavier controls, possible flare/rotation limits.
  • Aft CG: generally less stable, lighter pitch forces, reduced stall/spin recovery margin.
  • Use the actual airplane's current empty-weight data.
