Lesson 16 — Class B Airspace

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript, the existing study notes, and current FAA guidance.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Class B's defining VFR rule is simple and absolute: do not enter until ATC has explicitly cleared you into the Class B airspace. Everything else—shape, equipment, weather, student restrictions—supports operation around the nation's busiest airports.

Core idea

  • Class B surrounds many of the nation's busiest airports and is designed to contain high-density arrival/departure flows and published instrument procedures.
  • Each Class B is individually tailored; the common “upside-down wedding cake” is a memory picture, not a set of universal dimensions.
  • On VFR sectionals, Class B boundaries are shown with solid blue lines.
  • For VFR entry, an explicit ATC clearance is required before crossing the boundary.
  • Basic VFR weather minimum in Class B is 3 statute miles visibility and clear of clouds.

Shape and chart reading

  • Class B generally extends from the surface in the central core to about 10,000 ft MSL, with multiple shelves farther from the primary airport.
  • Each charted segment has a ceiling/floor pair, usually shown in hundreds of feet MSL. For example, “100/30” means ceiling 10,000 MSL and floor 3,000 MSL.
  • If you are below a shelf floor, you are not inside that Class B segment—but other rules such as the Mode C veil, ADS-B requirements, adjacent airspace, terrain, and speed limits may still apply.
  • The transcript's Memphis example uses a pilot at 2,500 MSL beneath a shelf whose floor is 3,000 MSL: the pilot is not yet in Class B.
  • Always use the current sectional/TAC rather than assuming every Bravo has the same rings or floors.

Entry clearance — the rule that matters most

  • You must receive an ATC clearance before entering Class B.
  • The transcript correctly contrasts Class B with C/D: merely establishing two-way communication is not enough.
  • You need wording that clearly authorizes entry, commonly “cleared into the Class Bravo airspace” / “cleared through the Bravo.”
  • A controller assigning a squawk code, altitude, heading, or saying your call sign does not by itself mean you may cross the boundary.
  • If you are approaching the boundary and have not received a Bravo clearance, remain outside and clarify with ATC.

Radio-call example from the lesson

  • The instructor gives the basic contact format: who they are → who you are → where you are → what you want.
  • Example structure: “Memphis Approach, Skyhawk 12345, [distance/direction], [altitude], inbound/requesting…”
  • ATC may assign a transponder code, altitude, route/heading, altimeter setting, and other instructions before actually issuing the Class B clearance.
  • Read back clearances/instructions containing critical numbers or routing so ATC can catch misunderstandings.
  • The example's most important teaching moment is that the pilot is still outside Bravo until the explicit clearance arrives.

Pilot certification / student-pilot nuance

  • A private pilot may operate VFR in Class B when all applicable requirements are met.
  • Student pilots can operate solo in some Class B airspace only after satisfying the specific training and logbook endorsement requirements of 14 CFR 61.95.
  • There is also a list of certain major Class B primary airports at which student operations are prohibited and at which the PIC must hold at least a private pilot certificate for takeoff/landing.
  • Do not reduce this to “students can” or “students cannot” fly Bravo universally; check the current rule and airport.

Equipment

  • An operable two-way radio capable of communicating with the appropriate ATC facility is required unless specifically authorized otherwise.
  • Unless authorized by ATC, operations in Class B require an operable 4096-code transponder with altitude reporting and ADS-B Out.
  • The Mode C veil generally extends 30 NM around listed Class B primary airports from the surface to 10,000 ft MSL and carries transponder/altitude-reporting and ADS-B requirements subject to regulatory exceptions.
  • The Mode C veil is not the same thing as the Class B boundary: you can be outside/below Bravo but still inside the veil.

Weather and ATC separation

  • Basic VFR weather minimum: 3 SM visibility, clear of clouds.
  • Class B provides VFR sequencing/separation services from other participating aircraft while radar services are available.
  • That does not relieve a VFR pilot of see-and-avoid, terrain/obstruction clearance, wake-turbulence avoidance, or remaining VFR.
  • If an ATC instruction would compromise terrain clearance, weather, or safety, tell ATC and obtain an amended instruction/clearance.

Operating near or under a Bravo shelf

  • You may operate beneath a Class B shelf without a Bravo clearance as long as you remain outside the Class B itself and comply with all other applicable rules.
  • Be careful not to “scrape” the boundary: altitude error, turbulence, rising terrain, or navigation error can create an inadvertent incursion.
  • Use VFR flyways/corridors and Terminal Area Charts where available when planning around complex Bravo airspace.
  • Expect dense traffic and wake turbulence near major terminal areas even when you are technically outside the Bravo.

Accuracy notes

  • The transcript says a pilot “has to have a private pilot certificate” and then gives the student exception. The complete rule is more nuanced and includes private, appropriately qualified recreational/sport pilots, and specifically endorsed students; use current 14 CFR 91.131 and part 61.
  • Class B's “surface to 10,000” description is general. Actual charted dimensions control.
  • Do not confuse a transponder code assignment or radar identification with the separate legal requirement for a Class B ATC clearance.

Worth watching — sectional shelves and the clearance example

  • The Memphis sectional example is useful for practicing ceiling/floor notation and deciding whether a specific altitude is inside or below a shelf.
  • The simulated radio exchange is worth hearing once because it demonstrates how several ATC instructions may be issued before the words that actually authorize Bravo entry.

What to remember for review

  • Solid blue boundaries; tailored upside-down-wedding-cake structure.
  • Explicit ATC clearance before entry.
  • 3 SM visibility / clear of clouds.
  • Two-way radio plus applicable transponder/altitude-reporting and ADS-B Out equipment.
  • 30-NM Mode C veil is broader than the Class B boundary.
  • Student-pilot operations require specific endorsements and are prohibited at certain primary airports.
  • You can fly beneath a shelf without a Bravo clearance if you stay outside the airspace and obey all other rules.
  • Read the actual chart; generic dimensions are only orientation aids.
