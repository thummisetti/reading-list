Lesson 59 — Aircraft Physiology & Risk Management

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Pilots are part of the aircraft system. Vision, oxygenation, balance organs, fatigue, stress, proficiency, judgment, and external pressure can all reduce safety margin before the pilot fully recognizes the degradation.
  • The lesson combines human physiology with aeronautical decision-making (ADM): understand the limitation, recognize it early, and use a structured response rather than optimism.

Vision and scanning

  • The eye's sharp central/foveal field is narrow, so effective traffic scanning uses small sectors with brief pauses instead of one fast continuous sweep.
  • The transcript describes a roughly 5–10° focused visual area and recommends scanning in small segments.
  • At night, central cone vision is much less useful for dim targets; rods in the peripheral retina are more sensitive.
  • Use off-center viewing: look slightly beside a dim object rather than directly at it, and continue a deliberate sector scan.
  • Night targets with little relative motion can be especially difficult to detect, reinforcing the need for disciplined scanning.

Night vision factors

  • Dark adaptation takes time and can be degraded by bright white light.
  • Use appropriate cockpit lighting and protect night adaptation before/while flying at night.
  • Hypoxia, smoking, some medications, fatigue, and illness can further reduce night vision; physiological limitations can matter before reaching regulatory oxygen altitudes.

Hypoxia

  • Hypoxia means inadequate oxygen availability at body tissues. In altitude-related hypoxic hypoxia, oxygen percentage remains about the same but lower atmospheric pressure reduces oxygen partial pressure available to the body.
  • Symptoms vary and may include euphoria, headache, impaired judgment, slowed reaction, visual degradation, poor coordination, drowsiness, or cyanosis. A dangerous feature is that judgment can degrade before the pilot realizes it.
  • The transcript discusses multiple mechanisms of hypoxia; for private-pilot operations, altitude-related hypoxic hypoxia is the most common focus, but reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, impaired circulation, or inability of tissues to use oxygen can also produce hypoxia.
  • Corrective action is to increase oxygen availability—use supplemental oxygen and/or descend as appropriate—and address the underlying cause.

Supplemental oxygen thresholds

  • Current §91.211 thresholds remain: above 12,500 ft cabin pressure altitude through 14,000 ft, required flightcrew use oxygen for the portion exceeding 30 minutes; above 14,000 ft, required flightcrew use it continuously; above 15,000 ft, each occupant must be provided oxygen.
  • Those are legal thresholds, not a promise that everyone performs well below them. Individual susceptibility, night operations, illness, fatigue, and smoking history can justify earlier oxygen use.

Carbon monoxide

  • Carbon monoxide is colorless/odorless and binds strongly to hemoglobin, reducing oxygen transport.
  • Possible symptoms include headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion, visual changes, and incapacitation.
  • In piston-aircraft operations, a cabin-heater/exhaust leak is a classic concern. If suspected, shut off the suspected heat source, ventilate with fresh air, use supplemental oxygen if available/appropriate, and land as soon as practical for evaluation.

Hyperventilation

  • Stress or anxiety can cause breathing faster/deeper than metabolic demand, lowering carbon dioxide and producing symptoms that can resemble hypoxia: lightheadedness, tingling, visual disturbance, or poor coordination.
  • Consciously slow and regulate breathing while maintaining aircraft control. Do not assume symptoms are “just hyperventilation” if hypoxia or another medical problem is plausible.

Spatial disorientation

  • The vestibular system senses acceleration rather than giving a perfect attitude reference, so prolonged turns, acceleration/deceleration, and head movement can create convincing false sensations when outside visual references are weak.
  • If visual references are lost, trust the flight instruments rather than bodily sensations and use trained inadvertent-IMC/ATC-assistance procedures.
  • Avoid abrupt head movements during instrument/low-visibility flight because they can worsen vestibular illusions.

IMSAFE — pilot condition

  • Illness
  • Medication
  • Stress
  • Alcohol
  • Fatigue
  • Emotion / eating (often taught as Emotion/Eating depending on source)
  • Use IMSAFE before flight and again when conditions change. Passing the legal minimum does not necessarily mean being fit to fly.

PAVE — whole-flight risk

  • P — Pilot: health, experience, recency, proficiency, ratings, fatigue, and personal minimums.
  • A — Aircraft: airworthiness, performance, equipment, fuel, loading, and your proficiency in that particular aircraft.
  • V — enVironment: weather, terrain, runway, airspace, night, airports, and route complexity.
  • E — External pressures: schedule, passengers, cost, desire to get home, events, reservations, or any pressure that makes continuing feel more important than maintaining margin.

Legal vs. proficient

  • The transcript gives a useful example: having a tailwheel endorsement may make the operation legally permissible, but if you have not flown a tailwheel airplane for a long period, proficiency can be poor.
  • A good ADM choice is to regain proficiency with an instructor instead of treating an old endorsement as proof of current skill.
  • This generalizes across aircraft type, night flight, crosswinds, instrument skills, short fields, mountain flying, and other operations: currency is not the same as proficiency.

Hazardous attitudes

  • Anti-authority: “Don't tell me.” Antidote: follow the rules; they are usually right.
  • Impulsivity: “Do something quickly.” Antidote: not so fast—think first.
  • Invulnerability: “It won't happen to me.” Antidote: it could happen to me.
  • Macho: “I can do it / prove myself.” Antidote: taking chances is foolish.
  • Resignation: “What's the use?” Antidote: I can make a difference.

External pressure and changing the plan

  • The transcript uses events/schedules as examples of pressure to launch or continue despite poor conditions.
  • External pressure can be subtle because the destination may feel important even when nobody explicitly tells the pilot to continue.
  • Build alternatives into the plan—delay, divert, overnight, drive, cancel—and make the change while options are still abundant.

Risk-management mindset

  • A risk factor does not need to make a flight illegal to deserve mitigation.
  • Use PAVE/IMSAFE to identify risks, then reduce likelihood or severity: add training, change time/route/aircraft, add fuel, wait for weather, take another pilot/instructor, or cancel.

What to remember

  • Small-sector scanning; at night use off-center viewing for dim targets.
  • Hypoxia can degrade judgment before obvious symptoms; legal oxygen limits are not personal-performance limits.
  • CO and hyperventilation can mimic other problems—recognize and respond conservatively.
  • Trust instruments over bodily sensations when visual references disappear.
  • IMSAFE = pilot readiness; PAVE = whole-flight risk.
  • Currency/legal privilege ≠ proficiency.
  • External pressure is a real hazard; keep an easy way to change the plan.
