Lesson 21 — Special Use Airspace

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript, the existing study notes, and current FAA guidance.

Special-use airspace is not one rule. The pilot must identify the specific category, determine whether it is active, understand what activity/hazard exists, and know whether entry is prohibited, requires authorization, or is merely strongly discouraged.

The six special-use categories in the lesson

  • Prohibited areas
  • Restricted areas
  • Warning areas
  • Military Operations Areas (MOAs)
  • Alert areas
  • Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs)
  • Prohibited and restricted areas are the main regulatory SUA categories. The others identify hazards/activity but do not impose the same blanket VFR-entry prohibition.

Prohibited areas — P-###

  • Prohibited areas exist for security or national-welfare reasons.
  • They are labeled with a P followed by a number and are charted where applicable.
  • Flight through a prohibited area is not permitted without authorization from the using agency / controlling authority.
  • The transcript's practical message is simple: unlike a MOA or inactive restricted area, you should not plan to casually transit a prohibited area.
  • Use chart information and current FAA publications for the lateral/vertical limits and any applicable authorization procedures.

Restricted areas — R-####

  • Restricted areas contain unusual, often invisible hazards to aircraft, such as artillery, aerial gunnery, missiles, or other military/test activity.
  • They are labeled with an R and identifying number.
  • The sectional gives the boundary; the chart margin / FAA SUA information lists altitudes, scheduled times, using agency, and controlling agency.
  • When a restricted area is active, nonparticipating aircraft may not enter without authorization from the controlling agency.
  • When it is not active/released to the FAA, the airspace may be available for transit without special permission, subject to normal ATC/airspace rules.
  • If status is uncertain, contact the controlling agency / Flight Service / ATC rather than assuming the published schedule proves it is inactive.

Using the chart-margin information

  • The transcript demonstrates looking up a restricted area's name/number, altitude limits, times of use, controlling agency, and frequencies.
  • Published times are not the same thing as a real-time activation guarantee. NOTAMs and ATC/controlling-agency status can modify or confirm activity.
  • The lesson's test-oriented rule is: inactive restricted area → transit may be permitted; active restricted area → obtain authorization before entry.
  • The transcript notes that frequencies above the normal VHF civil range shown for military use may be UHF frequencies not usable by typical GA radios; use the civil controlling-agency frequency provided.

Warning areas — W-###

  • Warning areas contain activity that may be hazardous to nonparticipating aircraft, similar in character to restricted-area activity.
  • They extend from the coast outward over domestic/international waters where the U.S. does not apply the same regulatory prohibition as a domestic restricted area.
  • They are labeled W followed by a number.
  • VFR entry is not prohibited merely because the warning area is active, but that does not make entry wise. Obtain activity information and avoid hazardous operations whenever practical.

Military Operations Areas (MOAs)

  • MOAs are established to separate certain military training activities from IFR traffic and to alert VFR pilots to concentrated military maneuvering.
  • Military aircraft may be operating at high speed, rapidly changing altitude/heading, flying formation, or performing abrupt maneuvers.
  • VFR pilots may legally fly through an active MOA without a special clearance, but should exercise extreme caution.
  • Before entering, obtain the MOA's activity status from the controlling agency/Flight Service when possible.
  • IFR traffic may be routed around an active MOA unless ATC can provide separation.

Alert areas — A-###

  • Alert areas identify high volumes of pilot training or unusual aerial activity.
  • They may contain intensive student training, aerobatics, or other activity deserving extra awareness.
  • All pilots—participating and transiting—share responsibility for collision avoidance.
  • No special VFR clearance is required solely to enter an alert area, but increase lookout and consider avoiding the area during heavy activity.

Controlled Firing Areas (CFAs)

  • CFAs contain activities that could be hazardous to nonparticipating aircraft, but the activity is controlled by observers/radar/spotters so it can be suspended immediately when an aircraft approaches.
  • Because the hazardous activity is stopped to protect transiting aircraft, CFAs are generally not charted.
  • The pilot normally does not need to identify or avoid CFAs in the same way as charted restricted/warning areas.

Category correction — National Security Areas are not one of the six SUA types

  • The first-pass notes grouped National Security Areas (NSAs) with special-use airspace. Current FAA organization places NSAs under “Other Airspace Areas,” not the six SUA categories above.
  • NSAs are covered in the next lesson. They request pilots voluntarily avoid flight for national-security reasons; temporary mandatory restrictions can be imposed by regulation/NOTAM when needed.

Preflight decision process

  • 1. Identify the SUA on the current chart.
  • 2. Determine its vertical limits and scheduled times.
  • 3. Check current status/NOTAMs and contact the controlling agency if needed.
  • 4. Decide whether entry is prohibited, authorization-dependent, legal-but-hazardous, or not operationally significant.
  • 5. If there is any doubt about hazardous military/firing activity, route around it rather than relying on the legal minimum.

Worth watching — sectional/chart-margin examples

  • The restricted-area example is useful for learning how the map label connects to the separate table of altitudes, operating times, controlling agency, and frequencies.
  • The warning-area/MOA examples help distinguish areas where entry may be legal from areas where the underlying activity can still make transit a poor decision.

What to remember for review

  • Six SUA types: prohibited, restricted, warning, MOA, alert, CFA.
  • Prohibited: do not enter without authorization.
  • Restricted: hazardous; active entry requires authorization, inactive/released may be transited.
  • Warning: hazards over water; entry not prohibited by the same mechanism.
  • MOA: military training; VFR may enter but use extreme caution/check status.
  • Alert: intensive/unusual activity; shared see-and-avoid responsibility.
  • CFA: activity stops for approaching aircraft; generally not charted.
  • Check actual activity/status, not just the printed schedule.
