Lesson 1 — The Four Forces

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — Private Pilot Ground Course, Lesson 1.

Core idea

  • Four forces act on an airplane in flight: lift, weight, thrust, and drag.
  • The airplane's behavior depends on the balance or imbalance between these forces.
  • In steady, unaccelerated flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag.
  • Changing one force changes the balance of the others, which changes the airplane's motion.
  • Understanding these relationships is a foundation for learning how airplane control inputs affect performance.

Lift

  • Lift is the upward aerodynamic force that supports the airplane and allows it to rise away from the ground.
  • The lesson introduces lift as being produced by two broad ingredients: relative wind and the design of the wing.
  • The wing shape used to produce aerodynamic force is called an airfoil.
  • The detailed explanation of how the airfoil produces lift is intentionally deferred to Lesson 2.

Weight

  • Weight is the downward force produced by gravity acting on the airplane and everything it carries.
  • Total aircraft weight includes the airplane itself, occupants, fuel, baggage, cargo, and anything else on board.
  • Weight acts downward through the airplane's center of gravity (CG).
  • The CG is the point around which the airplane's total weight can be considered concentrated.
  • The lesson's balancing-string analogy: if an airplane could be suspended from a point directly above its CG, it would balance there.
  • Weight opposes lift.
  • In steady, unaccelerated flight, lift = weight.
  • Center of gravity is only introduced here; it is covered in greater depth later in the course.

Thrust

  • Thrust is the force that propels the aircraft forward.
  • In a typical training airplane, thrust is produced by the engine and propeller working together.
  • Other aircraft can produce thrust differently; the lesson briefly mentions helicopter rotors as an example.
  • To accelerate forward, available thrust must overcome the drag opposing the airplane's motion.
  • In steady, unaccelerated flight, thrust = drag.

Drag

  • Drag is the aerodynamic force that opposes the airplane's motion through the air.
  • The lesson depicts drag as a rearward force because it opposes forward movement.
  • Pilots need to understand two major categories: parasite drag and induced drag.

Parasite drag

  • Parasite drag is drag that is not directly produced by the generation of lift.
  • The lesson uses the example of holding your hand out of a moving car: as vehicle speed increases, the airflow pushes your hand backward more strongly.
  • Parasite drag increases rapidly as airspeed increases; in the usual aerodynamic approximation it varies roughly with the square of airspeed.
  • This rapidly increasing drag is one reason a training airplane cannot simply keep accelerating indefinitely as power is added.

Three forms of parasite drag

  • Form drag: drag caused by the size and shape of an object moving through the air. Smooth, streamlined shapes generally create less form drag than blunt or irregular shapes.
  • Interference drag: drag created where different airflow streams meet around aircraft components and disturb one another, producing turbulence and less-efficient airflow.
  • Skin-friction drag: drag caused by friction between airflow and the aircraft's surface. Even a surface that looks smooth has microscopic roughness.
  • Painting, polishing, and keeping aircraft surfaces smooth can reduce skin-friction drag, but it cannot eliminate it completely.

Induced drag

  • Induced drag is drag created as a direct consequence of producing lift.
  • The lesson introduces the idea that airflow around the wing produces both lift and an associated drag penalty; the detailed mechanism is deferred to the next lesson.
  • Unlike parasite drag, induced drag decreases as airspeed increases under comparable flight conditions.
  • At low airspeed, the wing must work harder to produce the required lift, so induced drag is high.
  • At higher airspeed, less lift coefficient is required for the same weight, so induced drag falls.

Total drag and L/D max

  • Parasite and induced drag change in opposite directions as airspeed changes.
  • At low airspeed, induced drag dominates; at high airspeed, parasite drag dominates.
  • There is an intermediate airspeed where the combination of induced and parasite drag produces the minimum total drag.
  • The lesson refers to this region in terms of the airplane's lift-to-drag ratio, commonly written L/D.
  • L/D max is the condition where the airplane achieves its maximum lift-to-drag ratio, corresponding closely to minimum total drag for the configuration.
  • Many important performance speeds are related to this aerodynamic relationship, which is why the concept appears repeatedly later in training.

How the four forces interact

  • The four forces should not be thought of as isolated definitions; they are interconnected.
  • If the airplane is in steady, unaccelerated flight, the opposing force pairs are balanced: lift = weight and thrust = drag.
  • If one force changes without an immediately equal opposing change, the airplane accelerates in the direction of the resulting net force.
  • As the airplane accelerates or changes attitude, aerodynamic forces change too, so the system moves toward a new balance.

Flight demonstration 1 — adding thrust

  • The airplane is first trimmed for approximately level, steady flight.
  • The instructor increases engine power / thrust without initially moving the flight controls.
  • As the airplane accelerates, the wings generate more lift in the demonstrated condition.
  • Lift becomes greater than weight, so the airplane begins to climb even though the instructor has not deliberately pulled back on the controls.
  • The demonstration reinforces the central point: changing thrust can indirectly change lift and the airplane's vertical motion.

Worth watching if you want to see the airplane begin climbing after the power increase with the instructor's hands off the controls.

Flight demonstration 2 — increasing lift / raising the nose

  • The airplane is again established in approximately steady, unaccelerated flight.
  • The instructor raises the nose, increasing the wing's lift demand / angle of attack.
  • The increased lift production also increases induced drag.
  • Drag temporarily becomes greater than thrust, so the airplane slows down.
  • The observed airspeed decreases as the nose is raised.
  • The demonstration reinforces that increasing lift can increase induced drag and reduce airspeed if thrust is not increased enough to compensate.

Worth watching if the relationship between pitch, lift demand, induced drag, and decreasing airspeed is not yet intuitive.

What to remember for review

  • Four forces: lift up, weight down, thrust forward, drag opposite motion.
  • Steady unaccelerated flight: lift = weight; thrust = drag.
  • Parasite drag: increases strongly with airspeed.
  • Parasite-drag types: form, interference, skin friction.
  • Induced drag: comes from producing lift and decreases as airspeed increases under comparable conditions.
  • Minimum total drag / L/D max: occurs where the competing parasite- and induced-drag trends produce the best lift-to-drag ratio.
  • Most important conceptual takeaway: changing one force changes the balance of the whole airplane, so control and performance are about managing relationships between forces rather than memorizing four independent arrows.
