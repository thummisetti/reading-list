Lesson 34 — Digital Instruments

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • “Glass cockpit” systems present traditional flight, navigation, engine, and system information electronically rather than as separate mechanical gauges.
  • The transcript emphasizes that moving from analog to glass does not automatically require a special certificate endorsement, but system-specific training and familiarization are still essential.
  • The main pilot skill is understanding what the display means, where its data comes from, and how it fails.

Main components

  • PFD — Primary Flight Display: consolidates information traditionally shown by the six-pack: attitude, airspeed, altitude, vertical speed, heading, and navigation guidance.
  • MFD — Multi-Function Display: commonly shows moving map, engine/system information, terrain, weather, traffic, flight-plan data, and other pages depending on installation.
  • The lesson also discusses the sensor/computer architecture feeding those screens rather than treating the screens themselves as the instruments.

How to read a typical PFD

  • Attitude/horizon is central, with bank/pitch references similar to a conventional attitude indicator.
  • Airspeed is normally displayed on a vertical tape to the left; altitude on a vertical tape to the right.
  • Heading/HSI information is normally along the bottom, while vertical-speed information appears adjacent to the altitude tape.
  • Color bands reproduce familiar V-speed operating ranges; bugs and selected-value markers can be placed on target speeds/headings/altitudes.
  • Trend vectors can predict where airspeed or altitude will be in the near future if the current rate continues.

AHRS and magnetometer

  • AHRS — Attitude and Heading Reference System uses solid-state sensors to supply attitude and heading data without the traditional spinning attitude/heading gyros.
  • A remote magnetometer senses Earth's magnetic field and supplies a magnetic heading reference to the electronic system.
  • Loss/corruption of one sensor can affect multiple displayed parameters, so understand comparator/failure flags rather than assuming independent gauge failures.

Air Data Computer (ADC)

  • The ADC processes pitot/static pressure and often outside-air-temperature input to calculate/display airspeed, altitude, vertical-speed, and other air-data values.
  • A pitot/static blockage can therefore invalidate several digital indications at once just as it can on analog instruments.
  • “Digital” does not mean independent of the physical pitot/static system.

Navigation / HSI presentation

  • Glass systems often combine heading, selected course, CDI, bearing pointers, GPS/VOR source, and flight-plan information into an electronic HSI.
  • Always verify the active navigation source—GPS vs VLOC/VOR—before following a course indication.
  • Course guidance can be perfectly drawn but tied to the wrong source or waypoint; mode/source awareness matters more than screen appearance.

Failure modes and reversion

  • Know what red Xs, flags, comparator warnings, or data removals mean on the installed system.
  • Some two-screen systems have reversionary mode, allowing essential PFD information to be moved to the remaining display after a screen failure.
  • Know standby instruments, backup battery/power source, essential bus, and how long backup power is expected to last.
  • One screen going dark is different from loss of AHRS, ADC, magnetometer, GPS, alternator, or the entire electrical bus; the indications and remaining capability differ.

Automation and human factors

  • Flight directors and autopilots reduce workload only when the pilot understands the active modes and selected targets.
  • Monitor what the automation is actually doing; do not assume it captured the intended altitude/course just because a command was entered.
  • Moving maps are excellent situational-awareness tools but can invite head-down fixation. In VFR, maintain an outside traffic scan.
  • Know how to perform essential tasks without deep menu searching before flying the airplane.

Currency and training note

  • Database currency affects navigation procedures and displayed aeronautical data; verify the requirements appropriate to the type of operation.
  • Even if no separate endorsement is required merely because the instruments are digital, a pilot must still be competent with the specific airplane/equipment and any other endorsements/ratings required for that aircraft or operation.

What to remember

  • PFD = primary flight data; MFD = map/engine/system/auxiliary information.
  • AHRS = attitude/heading; magnetometer = magnetic reference; ADC = pitot/static air data.
  • One sensor failure can remove/corrupt several indications.
  • Know reversionary mode, standby instruments, and backup power.
  • Verify nav source and automation modes.
  • Glass improves information access; it does not replace an outside scan or system knowledge.
