Lesson 63 — Taxiing in Strong Winds

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Airplane wings and tail surfaces can still produce aerodynamic forces while the aircraft is on the ground. In strong wind, incorrect control positioning can let the wind lift a wing or tail enough to reduce steering or even overturn the airplane.
  • The control position must change as the airplane's heading changes relative to the wind.
  • The basic wing rule is: quartering headwind → aileron into the wind; quartering tailwind → aileron away from the wind.

Why aileron direction works

  • With a quartering headwind, turning the yoke/stick toward the wind raises the upwind aileron. That reduces lift on the upwind wing and helps prevent it from rising.
  • The transcript uses the memory idea “point your thumbs into the wind” for a headwind: yoke left for wind from the left, yoke right for wind from the right.
  • With a quartering tailwind, reverse the ailerons—turn away from the wind—so the wind is less able to get under the upwind wing and lift it.

Quartering headwind — tricycle gear

  • Aileron into the wind.
  • In the training-airplane examples in the transcript (Cessna 172 / Piper Cherokee style tricycle gear), the instructor teaches the elevator approximately neutral for the quartering-headwind taxi case.
  • The nosewheel and airplane's normal weight distribution provide ground steering; do not import the tailwheel airplane's full-aft elevator rule blindly into a tricycle-gear airplane.

Quartering headwind — tailwheel

  • Aileron into the wind.
  • The transcript teaches elevator aft for the conventional-gear/tailwheel example, raising the elevator so the headwind helps hold the tail down.
  • Keeping weight on the tailwheel helps preserve directional control; if the tail becomes aerodynamically light, steering authority can degrade quickly.

Quartering tailwind

  • Aileron away from the wind.
  • The transcript teaches elevator forward/down for both its tricycle-gear and tailwheel tailwind examples, helping prevent wind from getting under the tail and lifting it.
  • The classic memory aid is “climb into a headwind; dive away from a tailwind.” It is useful for aileron direction, with the elevator detail adjusted for the aircraft type/POH.

Wind directly ahead, behind, or across

  • As the wind angle changes continuously during taxi, smoothly reposition controls rather than waiting until the airplane has completed a turn.
  • With a direct headwind there may be little crosswind component demanding aileron deflection, but the elevator position still depends on aircraft type and POH technique.
  • With a direct tailwind, elevator-down/forward positioning is commonly used in the lesson's examples to reduce the tendency for the tail to rise.
  • With mostly direct crosswind, the aileron input is especially important because one wing is exposed to a strong lifting tendency.

High-wing vulnerability

  • The transcript emphasizes that high-wing airplanes can be especially susceptible to strong quartering tailwinds because the wing is elevated and can act like a large aerodynamic lever.
  • This is not a reason to ignore corrections in low-wing airplanes; all light airplanes can be affected by gusts.

Taxi technique in gusts

  • Taxi slowly enough to stop promptly and keep directional control.
  • Use rudder/nosewheel steering and brakes smoothly. Avoid excessive power against heavy braking.
  • Hold the appropriate control deflection continuously—do not let the yoke/stick float neutral just because the airplane is on the ground.
  • As wind strength increases, increase control deflection toward the full appropriate position.
  • If gusts are strong enough that control authority or braking/steering margin is doubtful, the safest choice may be to stop, reposition, secure the airplane, or wait for conditions to improve.

Relationship to crosswind takeoff/landing

  • The aileron logic is the same aerodynamic principle used during crosswind operations: position the ailerons so the wind does not lift the upwind wing uncontrollably.
  • During a takeoff roll, aileron deflection usually starts strongly into the wind and is reduced as airspeed/aileron effectiveness increases, following aircraft/instructor technique.
  • After touchdown in a crosswind, progressively increase aileron into the wind as speed decreases, eventually reaching full appropriate deflection while taxiing.

Aircraft-specific caveat

  • The transcript explicitly shows different elevator handling for a tailwheel vs. tricycle-gear airplane in a quartering headwind.
  • Use the actual airplane's POH/AFM and instructor technique. Landing-gear geometry, tail configuration, control locks, and manufacturer recommendations can change the details.

What to remember

  • Quartering headwind: ailerons into wind.
  • Quartering tailwind: ailerons away from wind.
  • Transcript headwind example: tailwheel elevator aft; tricycle-gear elevator neutral.
  • Transcript tailwind example: elevator forward/down.
  • Move the controls continuously as taxi heading changes.
  • Slow down or stop when wind exceeds practical taxi margin.
