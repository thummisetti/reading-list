Lesson 22 — Other Airspace

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript, the existing study notes, and current FAA guidance.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
“Other airspace” is a catch-all for operationally important areas and routes that are not simply another A–G class or one of the six special-use-airspace categories. Several are common DPE oral-exam topics because they test whether you can turn a sectional/NOTAM into a safe route.

Core categories covered

  • Local Airport Advisory (LAA)
  • Military Training Routes (MTRs)
  • Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)
  • Parachute jump areas
  • Published VFR routes: flyways, corridors, and Class B transition routes
  • Terminal Radar Service Areas (TRSAs)
  • National Security Areas (NSAs)
  • Air Defense Identification Zones (ADIZs)
  • Special Flight Rules / flight-restricted areas, including the Washington, DC SFRA/FRZ example
  • The underlying Class A–G airspace still exists underneath/around many of these; the additional route/service/security rule sits on top of it.

Local Airport Advisory (LAA)

  • The transcript begins with Local Airport Advisory, a Flight Service advisory service associated with certain Alaska airports.
  • The service can provide airport/weather/advisory information where applicable.
  • The instructor intentionally does not go deeply into it because it is Alaska-specific; pilots operating there should consult the current AIM/Chart Supplement for the service available at the specific airport.

Military Training Routes (MTRs)

  • MTRs support military low-level/high-speed training and are important to VFR pilots because military aircraft may be maneuvering at more than 250 kt at comparatively low altitude.
  • They are depicted on sectionals as thin/gray route lines with route identifiers.
  • VR identifies a route flown under VFR; IR identifies an IFR military training route.
  • A four-digit route number means no segment is planned above 1,500 ft AGL.
  • A three-digit route number means one or more segments may be above 1,500 ft AGL.
  • These numbering rules describe altitude potential, not whether the route is active right now.
  • When crossing or paralleling an MTR, increase visual scanning and obtain activity information from Flight Service/ATC when practical.

Temporary Flight Restrictions (TFRs)

  • A TFR is a temporary restriction established by NOTAM; it may not exist on the printed sectional you planned with.
  • Reasons include hazards/disasters, protection of persons/property, space operations, VIP/security movement, and major sporting/special events under applicable rules.
  • The controlling NOTAM specifies location, lateral/vertical limits, effective times, purpose, and who/what operations are permitted.
  • Do not assume every TFR means “nobody can fly there.” Read the actual NOTAM; permitted operations and procedures vary by TFR type.
  • The instructor demonstrates using the FAA TFR map/site and mentions Flight Service / 1800WXBRIEF and electronic flight-planning tools as practical sources.
  • Some recurring areas/events are depicted on charts with advisory symbols, but the symbol is not proof that a restriction is active. Check current NOTAMs.
  • A TFR can be created or changed after your initial planning, so recheck current NOTAMs close to departure and during longer flights when appropriate.

Parachute jump areas

  • Parachute operations create a collision hazard extending vertically through the jump/drop area, not just near the airport surface.
  • The transcript demonstrates the Chart Supplement parachute-jumping section, which can list approximate location, maximum jump altitude, and operating remarks.
  • Frequently used jump areas may also be depicted on sectionals.
  • But not every legal parachute operation is necessarily shown as a permanent charted area; NOTAMs/ATC/CTAF information and visual vigilance still matter.
  • Avoid transiting an active drop zone and listen for jump-aircraft advisories when operating nearby.

Published VFR routes

  • Published VFR routes help VFR traffic move around or through complex terminal airspace while reducing conflict with major IFR flows.
  • VFR flyways: recommended routes, typically depicted on VFR flyway planning charts associated with Terminal Area Charts. A flyway normally keeps you outside Class B, so no Bravo clearance is required merely to follow it.
  • VFR corridors: defined passageways through/adjacent to complex Class B structures where the surrounding Bravo is not part of the corridor. The transcript stresses reading the route's specific altitude/routing rules because an error can become a Class B incursion.
  • Class B VFR transition routes: charted routes through Class B, often depicted with prominent arrows. Because you actually enter Bravo, you still need the required Class B ATC clearance.
  • Published route graphics do not replace the underlying airspace rules. Know whether the particular route stays outside Bravo or requires a clearance through it.

TRSA — Terminal Radar Service Area

  • A TRSA is depicted by gray/black chart boundaries around certain terminal areas, often centered on a Class D airport.
  • The underlying airspace class remains whatever the sectional shows; a TRSA is not a separate A–G class.
  • Participation in TRSA radar services is generally voluntary for VFR pilots, though participation is encouraged.
  • If you participate, ATC can provide sequencing and radar traffic services appropriate to the TRSA.
  • The instructor calls this a common oral-exam identification question: recognize the charted TRSA ring and know that the service is useful but not itself a mandatory VFR-entry class.

National Security Areas (NSAs)

  • NSAs are established where increased security/safety of ground facilities is desirable.
  • They are charted with heavy dashed magenta boundaries and a note requesting pilots to avoid flight through the area.
  • Ordinarily the avoidance request is voluntary.
  • When greater security is needed, flight in the NSA can be temporarily prohibited/restricted under applicable regulation/NOTAM.
  • The transcript's durable takeaway is therefore: read the chart note and check current NOTAMs before assuming an NSA is merely advisory.

ADIZ — Air Defense Identification Zone

  • An ADIZ is airspace in which aircraft identification/location/control requirements support national security, especially around international/coastal boundaries.
  • Crossing an ADIZ can require an appropriate flight plan, operable two-way radio, transponder/identification procedures, position reporting, and compliance with current interception/security rules.
  • The transcript gives the Mexico/coastal example but explicitly says there are additional details beyond the short overview.
  • Do not use a ground-course summary as an international-border checklist. Check current AIM, customs/border requirements, NOTAMs, eAPIS/CBP requirements, and destination-country rules for an actual international flight.

Special Flight Rules Areas / Washington, DC SFRA and FRZ

  • A Special Flight Rules Area (SFRA) imposes special procedures beyond the ordinary underlying airspace rules.
  • The Washington, DC region is the high-consequence example: it includes a broader SFRA and a more restrictive Flight Restricted Zone (FRZ) around the core.
  • Operations can require specific training, flight plans, discrete transponder codes, communication, route/gateway procedures, and authorization depending on where you operate.
  • Never rely on a memorized old diameter or procedure for DC security airspace; use the current FAA security-flight guidance and NOTAMs.

Accuracy / terminology notes

  • The transcript says VR means “visual route”; FAA terminology is VFR Military Training Route, while IR is an IFR Military Training Route.
  • The transcript's MTR numbering concept is correct in substance: four digits = no segment above 1,500 AGL; three digits = at least one segment above 1,500 AGL.
  • Do not treat a chart symbol for recurring TFR/security activity as the legal restriction itself. The current NOTAM/regulation controls.
  • NSA restrictions do not literally transform the charted area into a permanent “prohibited area”; temporary flight prohibition/restriction is imposed through the applicable regulatory/NOTAM mechanism.

Worth watching — chart symbols/routes

  • MTR route identifiers and the three-digit/four-digit examples are easier to remember visually.
  • The VFR flyway/corridor/transition-route graphics are especially worth seeing because the legal difference hinges on whether the route remains outside or actually passes through Class B.
  • The TRSA gray-ring and NSA heavy-dashed-magenta examples are classic chart-identification items.

What to remember for review

  • MTR: VR/IR; four digits = all segments at/below 1,500 AGL; three digits = some segment may be above 1,500 AGL.
  • TFRs come from current NOTAMs; check them before every flight.
  • Parachute areas: use Chart Supplement/chart/NOTAM/ATC information and avoid active jumps.
  • Flyway = recommended path normally outside Bravo; corridor = defined passage through complex airspace; Bravo transition route = route through Bravo requiring Bravo clearance.
  • TRSA radar participation is generally optional for VFR but encouraged.
  • NSA usually requests voluntary avoidance, but current restrictions can make flight prohibited.
  • ADIZ/international-border operations have identification/flight-plan/equipment/procedural requirements.
  • DC SFRA/FRZ has special high-consequence procedures—always use current guidance.
