Lesson 7 — Airplane Controllability

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Controllability is the other side of stability: stability describes what the airplane tends to do on its own after a disturbance; controllability describes whether the pilot has enough authority to deliberately make it do something else.

Core idea

  • Controllability is the airplane's ability to respond to pilot control inputs and achieve the desired attitude or flight path.
  • A highly stable airplane is not automatically highly controllable; design requires a balance between resisting unwanted motion and responding adequately when the pilot commands motion.
  • Control authority depends on the aerodynamic force the control surfaces can create, so airspeed, CG location, power/slipstream, configuration, and airflow quality all matter.
  • The practical question is not merely “do the controls move?” but “can the controls generate enough moment to overcome the airplane's existing aerodynamic and weight moments?”

Control effectiveness and airflow

  • Ailerons, elevator, and rudder work by changing aerodynamic forces, so they require airflow.
  • At higher airspeed, greater dynamic pressure generally makes a given control deflection more effective and often increases control force.
  • At low airspeed, aerodynamic controls usually have less authority and larger deflections may be required.
  • Near a stall, separated/turbulent airflow can reduce effectiveness further, especially if a control surface lies in disturbed wing wake.
  • This is why low-speed operations demand more deliberate, coordinated control and why the airplane may feel “mushy” as it approaches a stall.

CG position changes controllability

  • The lesson carries forward the CG/stability discussion from Lesson 6: moving the CG changes the moment the tail must overcome.
  • Forward CG: usually increases longitudinal stability, but the tail must produce more balancing force and the pilot may have less available nose-up authority.
  • If the CG is too far forward, the elevator may not be able to create enough pitching moment to rotate for takeoff or flare adequately for landing.
  • The instructor uses the takeoff roll as an intuitive example: an excessively nose-heavy airplane may accelerate but refuse to rotate because the tail cannot generate enough downforce.
  • Aft CG: reduces the tail force required to balance the airplane and can make pitch response feel more sensitive, but it reduces natural stability and may reduce the ability to produce a strong nose-down recovery moment.
  • An aft-CG airplane can therefore be easier to pitch but harder to recover from a stall/spin; “more responsive” is not the same as “safer” or “better controlled.”
  • Always remain within the approved CG envelope in the POH/AFM.

Moment arms and control authority

  • Control effectiveness depends on both aerodynamic force and the moment arm between that force and the CG.
  • A force acting farther from the CG creates a larger pitching/yawing/rolling moment for the same force.
  • CG movement changes that lever-arm relationship. The instructor compares this to widening or narrowing your stance: geometry affects how much leverage is available to control balance.
  • This connects to the existing notes' useful moment relationship: moment = force × distance.

Power and propeller slipstream

  • Power can change controllability even before aircraft airspeed changes because the propeller accelerates a stream of air aft: the slipstream / prop wash.
  • If the elevator, rudder, or other control surface lies in that slipstream, increasing power increases local airflow over it and can increase its authority.
  • This is particularly noticeable in many single-engine propeller airplanes at low forward airspeed, such as takeoff, go-around, and stall recovery.
  • Not every control surface sits in propeller slipstream. Surfaces outside it will not receive the same immediate power-related increase in authority.
  • The instructor uses powered-lift / blown-wing aircraft as an extreme example: engine/propeller flow is deliberately directed over the wing and controls so added power strongly increases both lift and control authority.

Relative wind versus slipstream

  • The transcript loosely calls propeller slipstream “part of relative wind.” For study purposes, keep the terms separate.
  • Relative wind is conventionally airflow opposite the aircraft's flight path; propeller slipstream is locally accelerated airflow produced by the propeller.
  • Both contribute to the actual local airflow a control surface experiences, which is why power can affect control authority even at the same aircraft airspeed.

Stall recovery application

  • The lesson applies controllability to a stall: as the wing/tail airflow becomes disturbed and airspeed is low, control authority may be reduced.
  • Breaking the stall by reducing AOA remains the primary recovery action.
  • Adding power, when appropriate for the aircraft/procedure, can help by accelerating the airplane and by immediately putting more propeller slipstream over tail/control surfaces.
  • The instructor uses a “max, relax, roll” teaching phrase to emphasize power, AOA reduction, and restoring the desired bank after the stall is broken.
  • Do not treat that phrase as a universal checklist; use the POH/AFM and your instructor's approved stall-recovery procedure.

Adverse yaw and coordination remain part of controllability

  • Aileron deflection changes both lift and drag, so rolling inputs can create adverse yaw.
  • The rudder supplies the yaw control needed to keep the airplane coordinated while the ailerons control bank.
  • If airflow is weak, the rudder and ailerons may both feel less effective; coordinated inputs are especially important near low-speed/high-AOA conditions.

Trim and secondary/alternate control systems

  • The existing notes remain useful here: trim changes the control's aerodynamic balance/bias so the pilot does not have to hold continuous pressure after establishing the desired condition.
  • An out-of-trim airplane is usually still controllable, but pilot workload and required control force increase.
  • Some aircraft use stabilators, anti-servo tabs, balance tabs, servo tabs, spoilers, or combined surfaces such as ruddervators; the hardware varies but the core question remains how much aerodynamic moment the pilot can command.

Worth watching — CG/control-authority diagrams

  • The forward-CG sketches make the lever-arm problem intuitive: the farther forward the CG moves, the more tail force is needed to pitch the nose up.
  • The aft-CG discussion is useful for seeing the opposite tradeoff: less effort to pitch, but less restoring stability and less margin for recovery.

Worth watching — slipstream / blown-wing examples

  • The lesson visually shows which parts of an airplane are inside propeller slipstream and therefore gain immediate authority when power is added.
  • The blown-wing example exaggerates the principle in a memorable way: directing powered airflow over wings/control surfaces can dramatically increase lift and control effectiveness.

What to remember for review

  • Controllability = response to pilot input.
  • Controls need airflow; authority generally increases with dynamic pressure.
  • Forward CG: more stable but potentially less nose-up authority.
  • Aft CG: more pitch-sensitive/less stable and can impair stall/spin recovery.
  • Control moment depends on force × distance from the CG.
  • Power can increase control authority through propeller slipstream before airspeed has increased much.
  • Surfaces outside the slipstream do not get the same immediate boost.
  • Near a stall, reduce AOA first; added power can help restore energy and local control airflow as specified by the airplane's procedure.
  • Trim reduces sustained control force; it does not replace control authority or proper flying.
