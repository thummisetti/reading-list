Lesson 7 — Airplane Controllability

Core idea

  • Controllability is the airplane’s ability to respond to pilot control inputs and achieve a desired attitude/flight path.
  • Control authority varies with airspeed, configuration, power, and airflow over the control surfaces.

Control effectiveness and airspeed

  • At higher airspeed, greater dynamic pressure generally makes aerodynamic controls more effective and control forces heavier.
  • At low airspeed, controls are less effective and may need larger deflections.
  • Near a stall, disturbed/separated airflow can reduce control effectiveness significantly.

Adverse yaw and coordination

  • Aileron deflection changes lift and drag differently on the two wings.
  • The wing with the down aileron tends to produce more lift and more drag, yawing opposite the desired turn: adverse yaw.
  • Use coordinated rudder with aileron to keep the airplane from slipping/skidding.

Trim

  • Trim changes a small aerodynamic surface or control bias so the pilot does not have to hold continuous pressure.
  • Trim is used after establishing the desired attitude/power condition.
  • An out-of-trim airplane is still controllable, but workload and control forces increase.

Secondary/alternate controls

  • Many airplanes use trim tabs; some use stabilators, anti-servo tabs, balance tabs, or other systems.
  • Different aircraft may combine control functions—for example, V-tail ruddervators—but the underlying pitch/yaw functions remain.

Practical implications

  • Never judge control authority solely by control position; airflow is what makes the controls effective.
  • Low-speed operations, crosswinds, stalls, and go-arounds require deliberate coordinated control because control effectiveness and yawing tendencies can change rapidly.

What to remember

  • Controllability = response to pilot input.
  • Control effectiveness generally rises with airflow.
  • Trim reduces pressure, not the need to fly the airplane.
  • Coordinate aileron and rudder.
