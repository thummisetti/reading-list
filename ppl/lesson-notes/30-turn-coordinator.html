Lesson 30 — The Turn Coordinator

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The lesson compares the turn coordinator with the older turn-and-slip indicator.
  • Both use a gyro to show turn information and an inclinometer ball to show coordination, but their gyro axes are mounted differently.
  • A turn coordinator can sense initial roll rate/tendency as well as turn rate; a traditional turn-and-slip indicator primarily responds to yaw/turn rate.

Gyroscopic operation

  • A rapidly spinning gyro resists changes to its plane of rotation—rigidity in space.
  • Gyroscopic precession lets the instrument convert aircraft rotational motion into a pointer/miniature-airplane deflection.
  • The turn coordinator's canted gyro axis makes it responsive to both roll and yaw. The turn-and-slip gyro is mounted to sense yaw more directly.

Electrical power and redundancy

  • The lesson highlights the DC electric marking on the instruments in its training-aircraft examples.
  • In many legacy trainers, the attitude and heading gyros are vacuum-driven while the turn instrument is electrical. That gives useful redundancy if the vacuum system fails.
  • When electrical power is applied, the gyro can often be heard spinning up. Some units show a red OFF/failure flag if power is lost or the instrument is unreliable.
  • Do not assume every airplane is wired the same way—verify the installed power sources.

Turn coordinator vs. bank angle

  • The miniature airplane does not directly show actual bank angle like the attitude indicator.
  • Its deflection primarily represents rate of turn/roll tendency. A steep bank at high airspeed and a shallower bank at low airspeed can produce the same turn rate.
  • Use the attitude indicator/outside horizon for actual bank-angle information.

Standard-rate turn

  • A standard-rate turn is 3° per second.
  • At 3°/sec, 360° takes 120 seconds = 2 minutes.
  • Aligning the turn indication with the standard-rate mark indicates this turn rate; it is not a fixed bank-angle command.

Inclinometer ball — slip and skid

  • The ball is a separate mechanical inclinometer responding to gravity and lateral acceleration.
  • Ball centered = coordinated flight.
  • A ball displaced toward the inside of a turn indicates a slip; toward the outside indicates a skid in the usual turn interpretation.
  • Training mnemonic: “step on the ball”—apply rudder toward the displaced ball while maintaining the desired bank/flight path.
  • Cross-controlled skids near stall speed are particularly hazardous because yaw plus stall can lead to a spin.

Preflight/failure awareness

  • Verify the instrument is powered, flag-free, and responds in the expected direction during taxi turns when appropriate for the installed unit.
  • A failed turn coordinator can look stationary even while the airplane is turning; compare it with heading, attitude, compass, and outside cues.

What to remember

  • Turn coordinator = roll tendency + turn rate; turn-and-slip = primarily turn/yaw rate.
  • 3°/sec = standard rate = 360° in 2 minutes.
  • Miniature airplane is not a bank-angle display.
  • Ball centered = coordinated; “step on the ball.”
  • Many legacy turn instruments are electrically powered, but verify your airplane.
