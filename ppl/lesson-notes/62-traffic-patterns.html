Lesson 62 — Traffic Patterns

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • A traffic pattern is a predictable flow around a runway that lets arriving, departing, and training aircraft sequence safely.
  • The standard leg names are upwind/departure, crosswind, downwind, base, and final.
  • The goal is not a geometrically perfect rectangle. The goal is predictable position, spacing, communication, stabilized approaches, and collision avoidance.

Pattern direction

  • In the absence of a published/assigned exception, airplane traffic patterns normally use left turns.
  • Right traffic may be published for terrain, noise, obstacles, parallel-runway flows, or other local reasons.
  • Check the sectional airport symbol, Chart Supplement, segmented circle/traffic-pattern indicators where available, NOTAMs, and ATC instructions rather than assuming every runway uses left traffic.

Traffic-pattern altitude (TPA)

  • A common rule of thumb for light airplanes is roughly 1,000 ft AGL, but TPA is airport-specific.
  • The transcript gives an important example: adding 1,000 ft to field elevation would have suggested about 1,465 ft MSL, yet the Chart Supplement for the example airport published a TPA of 1,250 ft MSL—only about 785 ft AGL.
  • Therefore, do not compute a generic altitude and stop. Check the current airport data for the published/recommended TPA.
  • Larger/turbine aircraft may use a higher pattern altitude than light airplanes at some airports; local procedures can differ.

Pattern leg meanings

  • Upwind/departure: generally along the extended runway centerline after takeoff.
  • Crosswind: approximately perpendicular to the runway after the departure leg.
  • Downwind: parallel to the runway in the opposite direction from landing.
  • Base: approximately perpendicular to final.
  • Final: aligned with the runway centerline for landing.

Departing the pattern

  • After takeoff, climb on the departure leg while maintaining runway alignment and correcting for crosswind.
  • Turn crosswind at an appropriate safe altitude/position considering local procedures, traffic, and aircraft performance.
  • When leaving the pattern, use the published/standard departure technique and avoid turning across traffic unexpectedly.

Entering a non-towered pattern

  • A common recommended entry for an established pattern is approximately 45° to the downwind at pattern altitude, joining in a position that gives good visibility and spacing.
  • Observe wind indicators and traffic before entry. Listen to CTAF early enough to understand which runway/flow is in use.
  • If approaching from the opposite side of the airport, use a recognized safe entry technique rather than descending blindly through pattern traffic.
  • At towered airports, follow ATC's assigned entry and sequencing; ATC may ask for straight-in, base, downwind, or another entry.

Radio calls and visual lookout

  • At non-towered airports, self-announce position and intentions using concise, useful calls on the correct CTAF.
  • Radio calls do not create right-of-way and not every aircraft has or uses a radio. Keep looking outside.
  • Listen for aircraft whose reported positions do not match what you see; resolve uncertainty early.

Spacing and sequencing

  • Adjust speed and pattern dimensions to maintain safe spacing from traffic ahead.
  • Do not crowd another airplane so closely that you are forced into an unstable base/final or rushed landing.
  • If spacing collapses, extend a leg when appropriate or go around rather than improvising a tight maneuver.

Wind correction

  • Crab as needed on upwind/crosswind/downwind/base to maintain the intended rectangular ground track.
  • A tailwind on downwind increases groundspeed and can carry the airplane farther before the base turn than expected; a headwind on final changes the opposite relationship.
  • Pattern turn points should respond to actual wind and spacing rather than fixed landmarks alone.

Wake turbulence

  • Larger/heavier aircraft can leave strong wingtip vortices that sink and drift with the wind.
  • When following a larger aircraft, consider its rotation/touchdown point, flightpath, and wind drift; avoid operating in the likely wake path.
  • ATC spacing or another aircraft's landing does not guarantee the wake is harmless to a light airplane.

Base-to-final hazard

  • An overshoot of final can tempt a pilot to use excessive inside rudder while opposing with aileron to force the nose toward the runway.
  • That creates a skid; at high angle of attack it can produce an accelerated stall/spin at very low altitude.
  • Do not salvage a bad base-to-final turn. Reduce angle of attack as needed and go around when alignment or spacing is poor.

Stabilized approach mindset

  • Use the pattern to arrive on final with appropriate alignment, configuration, airspeed, descent rate, and spacing.
  • If the approach is not stabilized by the point specified by your training/aircraft procedures, go around rather than making increasingly aggressive corrections close to the ground.

Transcript caveat

  • The video treats many pattern conventions as general rules of thumb. Actual traffic direction, TPA, noise procedures, and local flow can be airport-specific.
  • Use current FAA airport data and local/towered instructions for the actual operation.

What to remember

  • Standard legs: departure/upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final.
  • Left traffic unless published/assigned otherwise.
  • Check actual TPA; do not assume 1,000 AGL.
  • Common non-towered entry: 45° to downwind at TPA when appropriate.
  • Maintain spacing and wind correction; radio does not replace see-and-avoid.
  • Never force a base-to-final overshoot with a skid—go around.
