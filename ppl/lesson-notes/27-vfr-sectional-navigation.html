Lesson 27 — VFR Sectional — Navigation

Comprehensive transcript-merged study notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course

Core idea

  • A sectional is both a map and a navigation database: visual checkpoints, populated areas, roads/railroads/power lines, terrain, navaids, frequencies, airspace, and obstacles can all help maintain position awareness.
  • The lesson introduces the two basic visual-navigation methods: pilotage and dead reckoning.
  • Good navigation combines chart interpretation with heading/time/fuel planning and continuous cross-checks rather than depending on one source.

VFR checkpoints

  • Flag symbols identify VFR checkpoints: prominent charted features selected because they are recognizable from the air.
  • They are not mandatory route points, but they can be useful for reporting position to ATC and for visual navigation.
  • The lesson's Signal Hill example shows how a call such as “4,500 over Signal Hill” gives ATC a clear geographic reference.
  • Choose checkpoints that are unique and easy to recognize rather than ambiguous terrain or small features that can disappear in haze.

Useful visual landmarks

  • Yellow shaded populated areas can be strong night references because the light pattern of towns/cities is often visible from the air.
  • Railroads, major highways, visible power lines, lakes/rivers, towns, airports, and distinctive intersections/convergences can make good checkpoints.
  • Terrain color/tint changes on the sectional help visualize valleys, ridges, and mountains; use the chart's elevation-color legend to interpret them.

Pilotage

  • Pilotage is navigation primarily by visually identifying landmarks and matching them to the chart.
  • The lesson example uses islands, converging railroad/power lines, and a town to keep updating position.
  • Do not stare at the chart: maintain an outside scan, verify more than one feature when possible, and account for how appearance changes with altitude, lighting, season, and visibility.

Dead reckoning

  • Dead reckoning predicts position from a known point using planned direction, speed, and elapsed time.
  • Lesson workflow: identify a known landmark → fly a planned heading and airspeed → after a calculated time, expect the next landmark → verify it visually → repeat.
  • Those planned landmarks/checkpoints become waypoints along the route.
  • Wind changes heading and groundspeed, so the full cross-country workflow later adds wind correction and time/fuel calculations.

VOR

  • VOR = Very High Frequency Omnidirectional Range.
  • A VOR provides azimuth information around a ground station. Directions extending outward from the station are called radials.
  • The sectional symbol/data box identifies the station name, frequency, and Morse-code identifier.
  • When relying on a VOR, tune the proper NAV frequency and identify the station rather than assuming the tuned signal is correct.

TACAN, VORTAC, and DME

  • TACAN = Tactical Air Navigation, a military navigation system.
  • DME = Distance Measuring Equipment. Compatible airborne equipment displays distance to the ground facility.
  • DME reports slant-range distance—the straight-line distance between aircraft and facility—not strictly horizontal ground distance. The difference becomes most noticeable high and close to the station.
  • A VORTAC combines VOR and TACAN functions. Civilian aircraft can use the VOR component and, if properly equipped, DME.
  • A VOR/DME is a VOR paired with DME capability.

Reading the navaid box

  • The box gives the facility name and NAV frequency.
  • Where applicable it also shows the DME/TACAN channel.
  • The printed Morse identifier tells you what station identification to listen for. Positive identification is part of using a ground navaid correctly.
  • When a navaid is physically located on an airport, the chart may depict its approximate location with a small dot while the adjacent navaid box identifies the facility type.

Compass rose and radials

  • The compass rose around a VOR depicts magnetic directions/radials for plotting.
  • The transcript describes the drawn rose in its example as extending about 10 NM from the station. Treat that as chart geometry, not the service-volume limit of the VOR.
  • Use the rose to visualize your bearing/radial from the facility and to plot courses on the sectional.

NDB

  • NDB = Non-Directional Beacon, an older radio-navigation facility.
  • An ADF-equipped aircraft can display the relative direction toward the NDB station.
  • The lesson notes that NDBs are being phased out, so many pilots may rarely use one; still recognize the symbol and basic operating idea.

Magnetic variation

  • Isogonic lines show magnetic variation, used when converting between true and magnetic course.
  • Keep the conversion convention consistent with your E6B/nav-log workflow and sanity-check that the resulting magnetic course/heading makes geographic sense.

Currency and failure-resistance

  • Navaids can be out of service. Verify current NOTAM/service information before depending on one.
  • Even with GPS, keep visual checkpoints and basic dead-reckoning awareness so a database, receiver, electrical, or signal failure does not erase your position awareness.
  • When a chart symbol is unfamiliar, use the current Aeronautical Chart User's Guide instead of guessing.

What to remember

  • VFR checkpoint flags mark recognizable position-reporting/navigation features.
  • Pilotage = navigate by visual landmarks.
  • Dead reckoning = known point + heading/speed/time → predicted next position.
  • VOR radials extend outward from the station.
  • DME is slant-range distance.
  • VORTAC combines VOR/TACAN; VOR/DME pairs VOR with DME.
  • Identify the navaid and check its status before relying on it.
  • Keep visual and dead-reckoning skills even when electronic navigation is available.
