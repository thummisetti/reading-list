Lesson 8 — Using Flaps

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Flaps are a secondary flight control that deliberately changes the wing's lift-and-drag characteristics. Their value is not simply “more lift”: they let the pilot trade airspeed, drag, descent angle, takeoff/landing distance, and climb performance in a controlled way.

Written-test emphasis / core idea

  • The instructor says there are two flap facts to “foot stomp” for the written exam: flaps increase lift capability and they increase drag.
  • Flaps are secondary flight controls, unlike the primary controls (ailerons, elevator/stabilator, rudder).
  • Extending flaps changes wing camber and, with some designs, effective wing area. This increases maximum lift coefficient and generally lowers stall speed.
  • At larger deflections, the drag increase becomes especially important, letting the airplane descend more steeply without allowing airspeed to build excessively.

What happens aerodynamically when flaps extend

  • Deflecting a trailing-edge flap increases the wing's camber.
  • For the same airspeed and AOA, increased camber generally produces a higher lift coefficient — but also a higher drag coefficient.
  • Because the wing's maximum lift coefficient rises, the airplane can support its weight at a lower airspeed before reaching the stall.
  • The transcript correctly notes an important nuance: saying “flaps increase lift” assumes you are comparing otherwise similar conditions. In actual approach flight the pilot commonly uses the extra lift capability to fly more slowly, so total lift still approximately balances the required flight-path forces.
  • Flap extension also changes pitching moment and trim requirement. The direction/magnitude of the pitch response depends on the airplane.

Lower stall speed and landing speed

  • The lesson uses a Cessna 172S example: roughly 48 kt stall speed flaps-up versus about 8 kt lower with full flap in the example.
  • Treat those numbers as the example airplane's values, not universal C172 or general-airplane numbers; use the actual POH/AFM.
  • The transcript connects approach/landing speed to the landing-configuration stall speed VSO and mentions the common training relationship of about 1.3 × VSO.
  • That 1.3 factor is a conceptual/reference relationship, not a substitute for the POH's specified approach speeds or adjustments for weight, gusts, runway conditions, or procedure.
  • The important aerodynamic point: flap extension gives a lower landing-configuration stall speed, allowing a slower approach/touchdown and generally less landing energy.

Why flaps steepen an approach

  • Flaps increase drag, so the airplane can descend at a steeper flight-path angle without accelerating as much.
  • They also permit a lower approach airspeed by increasing maximum lift coefficient.
  • Together, those effects make it easier to fly a slower, steeper approach and touch down at lower speed.
  • Large flap settings are often especially drag-producing, which can be useful when properly configured for landing.
  • Pitch and power still control the resulting path/airspeed; flaps do not automatically put the airplane on the correct glidepath.

Takeoff use

  • Partial flap can improve low-speed lift and may shorten ground roll or liftoff distance on some airplanes/procedures.
  • The instructor gives examples of training-airplane soft-field/short-field procedures that call for partial flap and mentions 10° on some Cessna models.
  • More flap also means more drag, so an excessive setting can hurt acceleration and climb performance.
  • The transcript also uses the C-130's takeoff flap setting as an illustration that the optimum lift/drag tradeoff is highly aircraft-specific.
  • The POH/AFM is controlling: use only the flap setting, speed, and retraction procedure specified for the actual aircraft and takeoff technique.

Accuracy note — don't memorize a universal “50% flap” rule

  • The video says that “most airplanes” are designed so the first 50% of flap travel produces more lift than drag and the second 50% produces more drag than lift.
  • That is a useful qualitative teaching idea — early flap increments often provide a favorable lift increase, while later increments tend to add proportionally more drag — but there is no universal aerodynamic 50% breakpoint that applies to all airplanes.
  • Likewise, the transcript's “use all the flaps if you're high” comment should not be treated as a universal procedure. Configuration changes must respect VFE, POH/AFM guidance, go-around considerations, wind/runway conditions, and stabilized-approach policy.

Flap types

  • Plain flap: a simple hinged portion of the trailing edge deflects downward, increasing camber and drag.
  • Split flap: only the lower portion of the trailing edge deflects, producing substantial drag while the upper wing contour remains comparatively unchanged.
  • Slotted flap: a slot between the wing and flap routes higher-energy air over the flap's upper surface, helping the flow stay attached longer and increasing maximum lift capability.
  • The instructor calls slotted flaps the most common type in contemporary use.
  • Fowler flap: a slotted-type flap that moves aft as well as down, increasing both camber and effective wing area before/while adding deflection.
  • Because Fowler flaps increase area as well as camber, they can produce a large increase in lift capability.

Configuration changes and pilot technique

  • Extending or retracting flaps changes lift, drag, pitching moment, and trim; expect to make coordinated pitch/power/trim adjustments.
  • Do not extend flaps above the applicable VFE or outside the approved flap operating range.
  • Abrupt flap retraction at low airspeed/close to the ground can remove lift suddenly and cause sink; retract according to the POH/AFM and procedure, especially during a go-around.
  • Full flap is not automatically best in every landing condition. Crosswind, gusts, runway length/condition, obstacle clearance, and aircraft-specific recommendations may lead to different settings.

Worth watching — lift/drag and flap-type graphics

  • The visual comparison of the wing before/after flap extension makes the camber change intuitive.
  • The plain/split/slotted/Fowler diagrams are worth seeing once because the geometry is easier to remember visually than by name alone.
  • The Fowler animation in particular shows the important aft-and-down motion that increases wing area.

What to remember for review

  • Flaps are secondary controls.
  • Flaps increase maximum lift capability and increase drag.
  • They generally lower stall speed in the extended configuration.
  • They let the airplane approach/land more slowly and can steepen the descent without excessive acceleration.
  • Partial flap can improve takeoff performance in aircraft/procedures that call for it; too much flap can degrade acceleration/climb.
  • Plain, split, slotted, Fowler are the major types emphasized.
  • Fowler flaps move aft and down, increasing area and camber.
  • There is no universal “50% flap” lift-vs-drag rule — use the aircraft's data.
  • Respect VFE and POH/AFM flap settings/retraction procedures.
