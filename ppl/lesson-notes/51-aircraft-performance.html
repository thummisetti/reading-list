Lesson 51 — Aircraft Performance

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Before flight, the PIC needs enough information to know whether the available runway and expected aircraft performance are adequate for the planned operation.
  • Performance changes with weight, pressure altitude, temperature, wind, runway slope/surface, configuration, and pilot technique.
  • Use the actual airplane's POH/AFM data and follow each chart's assumptions and correction instructions.

Preflight information

  • The transcript highlights §91.103: preflight planning includes runway lengths at airports of intended use and applicable takeoff/landing distance information.
  • Runway length alone is not enough. Compare it with aircraft performance for the expected conditions and leave an appropriate operating margin.

Pressure altitude and density altitude

  • Pressure altitude is altitude in the standard-pressure reference system; setting 29.92 inHg on the altimeter is a common way to conceptualize/read it.
  • Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature (with humidity having an additional smaller effect).
  • Higher temperature and/or lower pressure increase density altitude.
  • High density altitude means less dense air: normally aspirated engines generally make less power, propellers produce less thrust, and wings require more true airspeed/ground distance for the same indicated-air-speed condition.

High/hot/heavy effects

  • High elevation, high temperature, and high weight all work against takeoff and climb performance.
  • Expect longer takeoff roll, poorer obstacle clearance, lower climb rate, and often longer landing distance.
  • A runway that is comfortable on a cool sea-level day can become inadequate at high density altitude.

Reading performance charts

  1. Read the chart title, units, notes, configuration, and assumptions first.
  2. Enter with the required variables—commonly pressure altitude and temperature.
  3. Interpolate between published values when the chart calls for it.
  4. Apply weight, wind, runway surface/slope, or other corrections exactly as specified.
  5. Distinguish ground roll from total distance over a 50-ft obstacle.
  6. Sanity-check the result before using it.

Takeoff factors

  • Heavier weight increases acceleration distance and reduces climb performance.
  • A headwind generally reduces takeoff ground distance; a tailwind increases it. Apply only the POH's approved correction method.
  • Uphill slope and soft/grass/contaminated surfaces can increase takeoff distance.
  • Configuration and technique—flap setting, mixture, rotation speed, short/soft-field procedure—must match the performance data being used.

Landing factors

  • Higher weight and excessive approach speed increase landing distance and energy to dissipate.
  • Tailwind can dramatically increase groundspeed and landing distance.
  • Wet, contaminated, soft, or sloped runways can change stopping performance; use published data/corrections when available and conservative margins when data are limited.
  • Do not confuse landing ground roll with total distance over an obstacle.

Climb and cruise

  • Vx gives the greatest altitude gain per horizontal distance; Vy gives the greatest altitude gain per unit time. Published values vary with altitude, weight, and aircraft.
  • Climb charts may give time, fuel, and distance to altitude in addition to rate of climb.
  • Cruise charts relate altitude, power setting, RPM/manifold pressure, mixture, temperature, true airspeed, and fuel flow.
  • Use cruise data consistent with the actual power/mixture technique and conditions.

Book numbers are not guarantees

  • Published performance depends on stated aircraft condition, configuration, pilot technique, and atmospheric/runway assumptions.
  • Real airplanes, runways, winds, surfaces, and pilot execution vary. Add a sensible safety margin, especially where obstacles, high density altitude, short runways, or uncertain conditions reduce options.

What to remember

  • §91.103 makes runway/performance information part of preflight planning where applicable.
  • High / hot / heavy generally means worse takeoff and climb performance.
  • Read every chart's assumptions, units, and correction notes.
  • Ground roll ≠ distance over a 50-ft obstacle.
  • Use the actual POH/AFM and add realistic margin.
