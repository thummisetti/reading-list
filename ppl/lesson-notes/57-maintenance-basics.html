Lesson 57 — Maintenance Basics

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • This lesson focuses on determining whether an airplane is airworthy by combining a physical preflight with maintenance-record and inspection status.
  • For practical pilot use, airworthiness means the aircraft conforms to its approved configuration/required alterations and is in condition for safe operation.
  • The owner/operator has continuing maintenance responsibilities; the PIC still has to determine that the aircraft is acceptable for the intended flight.

Preflight vs. logbook review

  • A careful walkaround can reveal current condition problems, but it cannot by itself show whether required inspections, recurring ADs, or equipment checks are current.
  • The transcript therefore treats aircraft logbooks as a second half of the airworthiness check: know what entries and dates to look for.
  • For a rental/training airplane, learn how the operator documents inspection status and discrepancies rather than assuming somebody else checked it.

Annual inspection — §91.409

  • Most applicable aircraft need an annual inspection within the preceding 12 calendar months.
  • A 100-hour inspection does not automatically substitute for an annual unless it is performed by someone authorized to perform an annual and is entered as an annual.
  • Calendar-month logic means an annual completed on any day of a month is normally valid through the end of the corresponding month 12 calendar months later, subject to the aircraft's applicable inspection program.

100-hour inspection

  • A 100-hour inspection applies when carrying persons (other than crewmembers) for hire, or when giving flight instruction for hire in an aircraft the instructor/person provides, under the §91.409 conditions.
  • An annual inspection may substitute for a required 100-hour; the reverse is not generally true.
  • The 100-hour limit may be exceeded by up to 10 hours only while en route to a place where the inspection can be performed.
  • Any excess used for that purpose counts toward the next 100-hour interval; it does not reset the clock to 100 hours from the later inspection.

Other recurring checks

  • Depending on equipment and operation, additional inspection/test intervals can apply to items such as the ELT, transponder, altimeter/static system, and VOR equipment used for IFR.
  • Do not memorize one “AV1ATE” mnemonic and stop there; verify applicability for the actual aircraft and intended operation.

Airworthiness Directives (ADs)

  • ADs are legally enforceable FAA requirements issued to address unsafe conditions.
  • An AD may require a one-time action, recurring inspection/action, operating limitation, parts replacement, or other compliance method.
  • Maintenance records must allow the current status of applicable ADs to be determined, including method of compliance and recurring-action information where required.
  • Service bulletins and manufacturer recommendations are not automatically the same thing as ADs; their legal status depends on how they are incorporated into the aircraft's requirements/operation.

Engine/propeller time and overhaul recommendations

  • The transcript discusses engine/propeller overhaul times and makes an important distinction: many manufacturer TBO intervals are recommendations for typical Part 91 operations rather than automatically being a universal regulatory hard stop.
  • That does not mean “ignore TBO.” ADs, life-limited parts, operating rules, approved inspection programs, condition, insurance, and manufacturer requirements can change the answer.
  • When operating beyond a recommendation where legally permitted, maintenance history, condition monitoring, and mechanic judgment become especially important.

Maintenance-record information

  • Know where the airframe, engine, and propeller records are maintained and how to locate the last annual/100-hour and other required checks.
  • Records should document inspection status, maintenance/alterations, AD compliance, and other required continuing information.
  • Major repairs/alterations and equipment changes can affect weight and balance, operating limitations, and the aircraft's current approved configuration.

Pilot preventive maintenance

  • Part 43 Appendix A identifies limited preventive-maintenance tasks that an eligible certificated pilot may perform on an aircraft owned or operated by that pilot, subject to the rule's conditions.
  • The task must actually qualify as preventive maintenance, be performed using proper methods/data, and receive the required maintenance-record entry and approval for return to service.
  • Being legally eligible to perform a task does not mean it is wise to do so without the necessary competence, tools, and instructions.

Inoperative equipment

  • Do not reason from “the item is broken” directly to “the airplane is grounded” or “it is fine.” Follow the §91.213/MEL decision process.
  • Determine whether the item is required by the type-certification equipment list/KOEL, operating limitations, §91.205 or another rule, an AD, or the planned operation.
  • If operation is allowed with the item inoperative, deactivation/removal, placarding, maintenance action, and recordkeeping may be required.

Maintenance that changes flight characteristics

  • After maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration that may appreciably change flight characteristics or substantially affect operation, §91.407 can require an operational check by an appropriately rated pilot before carrying passengers.
  • Use the maintenance release/records and the aircraft's procedures to determine what is required before return to normal service.

Checkride approach

  • When an examiner asks “Is this airplane airworthy?”, do not answer with the annual date alone. Work through certificates, inspections, ADs, maintenance status, required equipment, discrepancies, and physical condition.
  • The current §91.409 text confirms the 12-calendar-month annual, the specified 100-hour applicability, and the limited 10-hour en-route extension.

What to remember

  • Annual: within 12 calendar months.
  • 100-hour: know exactly when it applies; annual can substitute; limited 10-hour extension only to reach inspection location, and excess counts forward.
  • ADs are mandatory; know recurring status.
  • TBO/service recommendations and regulatory requirements are not automatically identical.
  • Pilot preventive maintenance is limited and must be documented correctly.
  • For inoperative equipment, use the full legal decision process.
  • Logbooks + physical condition together support the airworthiness decision.
