Lesson 43 — Prog Charts Explained

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Prog means prognostic: these are forecast charts, not current observations.
  • They provide a fast synoptic picture of forecast pressure systems, fronts, precipitation, and broad flight-category/weather areas.
  • The key skill is reading the chart's valid time and legend before interpreting symbols.

Forecast vs observation

  • A prog chart depicts what forecasters/models expect at a future valid time.
  • Compare it with actual METARs, radar/satellite, PIREPs, and newer forecasts to see whether the forecast is verifying.
  • Never treat a forecast boundary as an exact wall; real weather transitions are spatially/time variable.

Pressure systems

  • H and L mark forecast high/low pressure centers, commonly with central pressure values.
  • Pressure pattern and isobar spacing help anticipate wind strength/direction tendencies.
  • Lows/fronts often correlate with more cloud/precipitation and changing weather, but the actual hazard depends on moisture, stability, season, terrain, and system strength.

Front symbols

  • Cold front: line with triangles pointing in direction of movement.
  • Warm front: line with semicircles pointing in direction of movement.
  • Stationary front: alternating triangles/semicircles on opposite sides.
  • Occluded front: triangles/semicircles on the same side.
  • Use the expected front position/time to anticipate changes in wind, ceiling/visibility, precipitation, turbulence, and convective potential.

Precipitation / weather shading

  • Forecast charts can use shading/hatching/colors to depict precipitation type/probability/intensity or broad weather areas.
  • The meaning is product-specific: always use that chart's legend rather than assuming a color means the same thing across weather products.
  • Precipitation areas can conceal important hazards such as low visibility, icing, thunderstorms, or mountain obscuration that require other products to assess.

Flight-category areas

  • Low-level forecast products may highlight broad regions where ceilings/visibility are expected to be below VFR thresholds.
  • Use such areas as a planning overview, then inspect detailed ceiling/visibility forecasts, METARs/TAFs, terrain, and trends.
  • Even an area forecast VFR can contain local IFR conditions, especially near terrain, water, precipitation, smoke, or nighttime fog-prone locations.

Valid times

  • Forecast charts are tied to specific valid UTC times/horizons.
  • For a flight between chart valid times, examine the trend/evolution across adjacent periods rather than assuming the earlier chart remains valid unchanged.
  • Older course examples may show fixed 12/24/36/48-hour chart arrangements; use whatever current forecast periods the modern product provides.

How to use a prog chart in a go/no-go workflow

  • 1. Check issuance/valid time.
  • 2. Find route relative to forecast highs/lows/fronts and precipitation.
  • 3. Identify broad ceiling/visibility risk.
  • 4. Ask what conditions are likely at departure, en route, destination, and diversion airports.
  • 5. Validate the broad picture with current observations, TAFs/GFA, radar/satellite, PIREPs, advisories, and winds.

Current-product note

  • The transcript walks through a particular aviationweather.gov interface. That interface can change; the durable knowledge is forecast meaning, symbols, valid time, trend, and cross-checking.
  • Modern graphical forecast products can overlap or replace older standalone chart presentations. Read the current product legend/documentation.

What to remember

  • Prog = forecast, not observation.
  • Read valid time first.
  • Know front symbols and H/L.
  • Colors/shading require the product legend.
  • Use prog charts for the big picture, then validate with detailed/current products.
