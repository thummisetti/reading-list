Lesson 35 — Don't Be Afraid of ATC!

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • ATC is a safety resource, not something to avoid because radio work feels intimidating.
  • Good radio technique is mostly listen first, know what you want to say, speak clearly, and respond accurately.
  • Perfect jargon is less important than unambiguous communication. If you do not understand something, ask.

What ATC does

  • Controllers sequence and separate participating traffic, issue safety alerts and traffic information, provide routing/heading/altitude instructions as appropriate, and coordinate aircraft between sectors/facilities.
  • Radar surveillance is aided by aircraft transponders, which make targets easier to identify and can report pressure altitude when altitude-reporting equipment is operating.
  • VFR radar services are valuable but do not eliminate the pilot's responsibility to see and avoid other traffic in VMC.

Transponder basics from the lesson

  • The four-digit transponder code is the squawk code. ATC may assign a discrete code so the aircraft can be correlated with its radar target.
  • Common modes include standby and altitude-reporting/Mode C-capable operation; modern installations may provide Mode S/ADS-B functions as well.
  • IDENT temporarily highlights the aircraft's radar return when ATC says “ident.” Do not press IDENT merely because you changed codes unless requested.
  • Know the emergency codes commonly taught to pilots: 7500 hijacking, 7600 radio failure, 7700 emergency. Use them in accordance with the actual situation/procedure.

Before transmitting

  • Listen long enough to learn the frequency rhythm and avoid stepping on an existing transmission.
  • Think through the call before keying the microphone.
  • Use the correct facility/frequency and, at a terminal airport, obtain ATIS/AWOS/ASOS first when available.
  • If the frequency is busy, keep routine calls especially concise.

Initial-call structure

  • A useful framework is who you're calling → who you are → where you are → what you want.
  • Example structure: “Little Rock Approach, Cherokee 7348 Juliet, [position/altitude], request [service/destination].”
  • On the very first call to a busy controller, it can be useful to establish contact before unloading a long request; follow local/controller practice.
  • Give aircraft type when it materially helps the controller understand your performance/capability.

Readbacks

  • Read back safety-critical clearances/instructions such as runway assignments/hold-short instructions, headings, altitudes, transponder codes, and other items when required or necessary to verify understanding.
  • Hearback/readback is a safety loop: speaking the instruction gives the controller a chance to catch a misunderstanding.
  • “Roger” means the transmission was received; it is not a substitute for a readback when a readback is needed.

If you did not understand

  • Say “say again”, request clarification, or state the part you did not understand.
  • If an instruction cannot be safely or legally complied with, say “unable” and coordinate another option.
  • ATC cannot know your capability, weather situation, equipment problem, or discomfort unless you tell them.

Useful terms

  • Roger: received your last transmission.
  • Wilco: received, understand, and will comply.
  • Stand by: wait; the controller will return to you. It is not a clearance to transmit immediately.
  • Affirmative / negative: yes / no.
  • Plain English is appropriate when standardized phraseology is not enough to communicate clearly.

Handoffs and frequency changes

  • During radar service, one controller may hand you to another frequency. Read back/acknowledge the new frequency as appropriate, switch, listen, then check in.
  • A check-in normally includes the facility name, callsign, and relevant altitude/status information requested by the situation.
  • If you lose contact or suspect you copied a frequency incorrectly, return to the previous frequency or use another appropriate published frequency rather than continuing silently.

Sample calls are patterns, not scripts

  • The transcript contains realistic controller/pilot examples. Keep the information structure, not every exact word.
  • Actual phraseology varies with airspace, workload, local procedures, and what ATC needs. Current AIM/controller instructions take precedence over memorized dialogue.

What to remember

  • Listen → think → key mic → concise call.
  • Who / who / where / what.
  • Know basic transponder/IDENT concepts and emergency codes.
  • Read back critical instructions.
  • Ask “say again” when unsure; say “unable” when necessary.
  • VFR radar service does not remove see-and-avoid responsibility.
