Lesson 40 — TAFs & METARs

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • METAR reports observed surface weather; TAF forecasts terminal weather for a stated valid period.
  • The instructor's key technique is to decode in a consistent group order instead of attacking unfamiliar abbreviations randomly.
  • For flight decisions, decode the report and compare it with nearby observations, forecast changes, radar/satellite/GFA, PIREPs, advisories, and trends.

METAR structure

  • Typical order: report type → station → date/time Z → wind → visibility → RVR if present → weather → sky condition → temperature/dew point → altimeter → remarks.
  • Station: four-character ICAO identifier in the contiguous U.S., commonly K + three-letter airport identifier.
  • Time: DDHHMMZ = day of month, hour, minute UTC.
  • AUTO indicates an automated report; COR indicates a corrected report when present.

Wind

  • Example 22012G20KT = wind from 220° true at 12 knots, gusting 20.
  • VRB means variable direction; a separate directional-variation group such as 180V260 can show the range.
  • METAR wind direction is referenced to true north; runway alignment/ATC wind use may be encountered in magnetic context, so know which product/source you are reading.
  • Strong gust spread and crosswind components matter operationally even when the airport remains legally VFR.

Visibility / RVR

  • U.S. METAR prevailing visibility is commonly in statute miles (SM); fractions and values such as P6SM can appear.
  • Runway Visual Range groups may appear at equipped airports and are runway-specific.
  • Do not reduce visibility assessment to a single number: precipitation, fog/haze, sun angle, terrain, and night conditions can make visual navigation harder than the nominal visibility suggests.

Present-weather codes

  • Weather groups combine intensity/proximity + descriptor + phenomenon.
  • Examples: -RA light rain, +TSRA heavy thunderstorm rain, BR mist, FG fog, SN snow, HZ haze.
  • Learn common roots and parse from left to right rather than memorizing every possible combination as a whole word.

Clouds / ceiling

  • FEW = 1–2 eighths; SCT = 3–4; BKN = 5–7; OVC = 8 eighths.
  • Cloud-base numbers are hundreds of feet AGL: BKN025 = broken at 2,500 ft AGL.
  • A ceiling is the height of the lowest BKN or OVC layer, or vertical visibility into an obscuration. FEW/SCT alone do not define a ceiling.
  • VV indicates vertical visibility when the sky is obscured.
  • Convective cloud identifiers such as CB/TCU are significant even if the raw ceiling/visibility looks acceptable.

Temperature / dew point

  • Temperature and dew point are in °C; M means minus.
  • A narrowing T–Td spread suggests increasing saturation/fog/cloud potential, especially with favorable cooling/moisture/wind conditions.

Altimeter / remarks

  • A2992 = 29.92 inHg.
  • Remarks can contain important operational weather details: peak wind, pressure tendency, lightning/thunderstorm location/movement, variable ceiling/visibility, precise temperature/dew point, precipitation amounts, and sensor information.
  • Do not automatically stop reading at the altimeter group if the weather situation is significant.

TAF structure

  • A TAF identifies the terminal, issue time, valid period, and the forecast wind/visibility/weather/cloud conditions.
  • TAFs cover the terminal vicinity and are not route-wide forecasts.
  • Decode the initial conditions first, then walk chronologically through every change group that overlaps departure/arrival time.

TAF change groups

  • FM (“from”) begins a new prevailing forecast, often representing a relatively rapid transition; subsequent conditions replace the prior prevailing set.
  • TEMPO describes temporary fluctuations expected during its stated period.
  • PROB30 when used expresses a probability of specified conditions during the stated period.
  • Some legacy examples/course materials may emphasize older formatting conventions; learn the meaning from the current product, not a frozen web layout.

Flight categories

  • VFR/MVFR/IFR/LIFR color categories are useful quick-look summaries based on ceiling and visibility thresholds.
  • They are not a go/no-go decision by themselves. A VFR category can coexist with strong crosswind, thunderstorms nearby, icing aloft, mountain obscuration, or conditions below personal minimums.

Accuracy/current-product note

  • The transcript demonstrates a particular website interface. Keep the decoding knowledge, but do not memorize menu locations or assume a third-party app is itself the originator of the observations/forecasts.
  • Use current authoritative aviation-weather data and confirm issue/valid times. Weather products and interfaces evolve.

What to remember

  • METAR = observed; TAF = forecast.
  • Decode METAR in group order.
  • BKN/OVC/VV can define ceiling; FEW/SCT do not.
  • Wind is true in METAR/TAF.
  • Read remarks when relevant.
  • For TAFs, follow all change groups across your operational time window.
  • One METAR/TAF is only part of the weather picture.
