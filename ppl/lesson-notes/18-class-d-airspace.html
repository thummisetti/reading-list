Lesson 18 — Class D Airspace

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript, the existing study notes, and current FAA guidance.

Class D is controlled airspace around an operating control tower. The key VFR entry requirement is the same communication concept as Class C: establish two-way radio communication before crossing the boundary.

Core idea

  • Class D generally surrounds a smaller airport with an operating control tower.
  • It is charted with a segmented/dashed blue line.
  • Before VFR entry, establish two-way radio communication with the tower and maintain communication while operating in the airspace.
  • Basic VFR weather minimum: 3 SM visibility, 500 ft below / 1,000 ft above / 2,000 ft horizontal from clouds.

Typical shape and vertical limits

  • Class D commonly extends from the surface to about 2,500 ft above airport elevation, but each area is individually tailored.
  • The sectional normally prints the Class D ceiling in hundreds of feet MSL inside a small boxed/bracketed number.
  • Instrument-procedure extensions or irregular boundaries can make the shape different from a simple circle.
  • The transcript describes a generic cylinder and even says “5 statute mile radius”; treat that only as an illustration. The charted boundary controls.

Blue versus magenta segmented boundaries

  • A segmented blue line identifies a Class D surface area.
  • A segmented magenta line identifies Class E airspace designated to the surface, not Class D.
  • The lesson calls out this color distinction because both boundary styles can visually resemble dashed circles around an airport.

Communication requirement

  • As with Class C, you need two-way radio communication established before entry, not a separate “cleared into Delta” phrase.
  • If the tower responds using your aircraft identification, communication is established unless the controller specifically tells you to remain outside.
  • If the controller answers only “aircraft calling, standby” without identifying you, remain outside until your aircraft is identified in the response.
  • Plan the initial call early enough to avoid crossing the boundary while waiting for a reply.

Arrival / tower communication example

  • The transcript walks through a normal towered-airport arrival rather than treating Class D as only a chart-memory exercise.
  • Useful initial information includes aircraft ID/type, position, altitude, ATIS information, and intentions/request.
  • Tower may issue traffic instructions, pattern entry, runway assignment, sequencing, and eventually a landing clearance.
  • If tower points out traffic—such as the transcript's Piper Cub on short final—actively look for it and report “traffic in sight” when you actually see it.
  • Landing clearance and runway assignment should be read back with enough information to remove ambiguity; runway hold-short instructions require complete readback under ATC procedures.
  • A landing clearance does not eliminate your obligation to maintain safe spacing, wake awareness, runway awareness, and go around if the approach becomes unsafe.

Weather and Special VFR

  • Normal basic VFR in Class D uses the familiar 3-152 minimums.
  • Within the lateral boundaries of controlled airspace designated to the surface for an airport, ordinary VFR generally also requires at least a 1,000-ft ceiling, unless operating under an applicable Special VFR clearance.
  • Special VFR may be available when the regulatory and ATC conditions are met; it requires an ATC clearance and has its own visibility/cloud and nighttime pilot/equipment requirements.
  • Do not treat Special VFR as a routine way for a low-experience pilot to “get around” poor weather.

Equipment and speed

  • A two-way radio is the defining equipment requirement for ordinary VFR entry into active Class D.
  • Unlike Class C, a transponder/ADS-B is not automatically required solely because an airplane is in Class D; other overlapping rules/airspace can still require them.
  • At or below 2,500 ft AGL within 4 NM of the primary Class C or D airport, the general speed limit is 200 KIAS.

Part-time tower / changing airspace status

  • Some Class D towers are not open 24 hours.
  • When the tower closes, the Class D surface area may revert to Class E or Class G, depending on the published designation.
  • The Chart Supplement states tower operating hours and the airspace status outside those hours; NOTAMs can change availability temporarily.
  • Therefore, a dashed blue circle printed on the sectional does not prove Class D is active right now.

Accuracy notes

  • The transcript's generic “5 statute mile radius” is not the FAA's governing definition. Actual Class D areas are individually tailored and commonly described by the published boundary, not a universal radius.
  • Class D's top is generally described as about 2,500 ft above airport elevation, charted in MSL; do not read “2,500 AGL” as a universal charted ceiling number.
  • The existence of a tower is what normally drives the active Class D surface area; when tower status changes, verify the current classification before applying Class D entry rules.

Worth watching — boundary colors and arrival calls

  • The side-by-side blue-dashed Class D versus magenta-dashed surface Class E examples are worth seeing once.
  • The tower-arrival sequence is useful for hearing how traffic advisories, pattern/approach sequencing, and landing clearance build on the basic two-way-communication entry requirement.

What to remember for review

  • Dashed/segmented blue boundary.
  • Usually surface to about 2,500 ft above airport elevation; exact chart controls.
  • Two-way communication before entry.
  • 3 SM / 500 below / 1,000 above / 2,000 horizontal.
  • Blue dashed = D; magenta dashed = surface E.
  • 200 KIAS general limit within 4 NM / 2,500 AGL of the primary C/D airport.
  • Part-time Class D can become E or G when tower closes—check Chart Supplement/NOTAMs.
