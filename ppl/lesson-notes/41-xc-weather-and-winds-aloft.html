Lesson 41 — X/C Weather & Winds Aloft

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Cross-country weather planning must cover the whole route and the whole time window: departure, climb, en route, destination, possible diversion/alternate, and trends.
  • Winds and temperatures aloft directly affect heading, groundspeed, time, fuel, turbulence exposure, and aircraft performance.
  • A weather app can aggregate products, but the pilot still needs to understand the underlying observations/forecasts and their limitations.

Start broad, then zoom in

  • Begin with the large-scale pattern: highs/lows, fronts, pressure gradient, major precipitation/convective areas, and broad ceiling/visibility trends.
  • Then examine route-specific products: METARs/TAFs, graphical forecasts, radar/satellite, advisories, PIREPs, winds/temperatures aloft, and NOTAM-related weather/nav issues.
  • Compare observed vs forecast. If actual weather is already worse or developing faster than forecast, update the mental model rather than clinging to the earlier forecast.

Winds/temperatures aloft

  • Forecast winds aloft are expressed relative to true north and in knots at standard forecast levels.
  • Use the forecast nearest the planned altitude/time/location, interpolating cautiously when the flight sits between levels/stations.
  • Temperature data help with density-altitude/performance planning, icing awareness, and true-airspeed calculations.
  • Wind forecasts feed the nav log: wind-correction angle → true heading → magnetic heading, and wind vector → groundspeed → time/fuel.

Traditional coded winds/temps groups

  • A common legacy format encodes direction by two digits (tens of degrees), speed by two digits, then temperature where applicable.
  • 9900 indicates light/variable winds under the product convention.
  • For winds 100–199 kt, the encoding adds 50 to the direction code and subtracts 100 from the written speed: to decode, subtract 50 from the direction code and add 100 to speed.
  • Example concept: a code beginning 73 with speed 45 represents direction 230° and speed 145 kt before any temperature digits.
  • Do not rely on handwritten-code memory if a modern decoded product is available; the skill is mainly useful for exams and understanding source data.

Choosing a cruise altitude

  • Weather can make a theoretically efficient altitude undesirable: stronger headwind, turbulence, icing, cloud layers, poor terrain clearance, or oxygen/performance constraints may dominate.
  • A higher altitude may provide a stronger tailwind or smoother air, but climb time/fuel and performance must be included.
  • A lower altitude may avoid winds or icing but increase terrain/obstacle exposure and turbulence from surface heating/terrain.

Crosswind/headwind interpretation

  • The wind direction tells where the wind is coming from.
  • A direct headwind reduces groundspeed; tailwind increases it; crosswind changes required heading and can also alter groundspeed.
  • Do not subtract/add full wind speed unless the wind is directly aligned with course—use vector/E6B calculations for real planning.

Hazards along the route

  • Check convective activity, icing/freezing level, turbulence, mountain obscuration, low ceilings/visibility, gusty surface winds, and wind shear—not just “is it VFR at destination?”
  • A route can be VFR at both endpoints and still be unsafe because of terrain, cloud layers, thunderstorms, smoke/haze, or winds in between.
  • PIREPs are especially valuable for validating actual tops/bases, turbulence, icing, and conditions between reporting stations.

In-flight updates

  • Weather planning continues after takeoff. Recheck weather through ATC/FSS/approved datalink sources as appropriate.
  • Datalink weather is excellent strategic information but is delayed; do not use delayed radar imagery for tactical thunderstorm penetration.
  • Keep diversion options and personal minimums active throughout the flight.

Current-product note

  • The transcript demonstrates particular websites/apps. The durable skill is understanding the products and data, not memorizing a menu path.
  • Modern graphical products may present winds/temperatures and route weather differently from older text pages. Always read product legends, valid times, altitudes, and update times.

What to remember

  • Cross-country weather is route + time + trend.
  • Winds aloft are true and drive heading/GS/time/fuel calculations.
  • Know 9900 and the 100+ kt coding trick for written-test decoding.
  • Compare actual observations to forecasts.
  • Endpoints alone do not describe route safety.
  • Datalink weather is strategic, not tactical thunderstorm radar.
