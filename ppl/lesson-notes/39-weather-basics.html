Lesson 39 — Weather Basics

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Most weather affecting light-aircraft operations occurs in the troposphere and is driven by unequal solar heating, pressure differences, moisture, stability, and lifting.
  • The practical goal is not memorizing meteorology vocabulary: it is recognizing what combinations create low ceilings/visibility, turbulence, thunderstorms, icing, wind shear, and poor performance.

Atmosphere and standard atmosphere

  • Earth's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen with variable water vapor and trace gases.
  • The troposphere begins at the surface and varies in depth by latitude/season; most clouds and weather occur there.
  • The standard atmosphere is a reference model used for altimetry/performance. Standard sea-level values commonly used in pilot calculations are approximately 29.92 inHg / 1013.25 hPa and 15°C.
  • Actual atmosphere rarely matches the standard model, so pressure altitude, density altitude, true altitude, and performance can differ from simple indicated values.

Unequal heating and circulation

  • Land/water, latitude, cloud cover, terrain, and time of day heat differently.
  • Warm air becomes less dense and tends to rise; cooler/denser air tends to sink, helping create pressure differences and circulation.
  • Those pressure differences start air moving; Earth's rotation and surface friction alter the final wind direction/speed.

Pressure systems and wind

  • Air tends to move from higher pressure toward lower pressure because of the pressure-gradient force.
  • Closely spaced isobars indicate a stronger pressure gradient and usually stronger winds.
  • In the Northern Hemisphere, flow around surface lows is generally counterclockwise/inward and around highs clockwise/outward; friction makes surface wind cross isobars more than winds aloft.
  • Wind changes with altitude because surface friction decreases and pressure patterns vary.

Moisture, relative humidity, and dew point

  • Water exists in the atmosphere as vapor, liquid droplets, and ice.
  • Dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled (at essentially constant pressure/water-vapor content) to reach saturation.
  • A small temperature–dew-point spread means the air is closer to saturation and raises concern for fog, cloud, dew/frost, and reduced visibility.
  • Cooling, adding moisture, or lifting air can bring it to saturation.

Cloud formation / lifting mechanisms

  • Clouds form when moist air is lifted/cooled to saturation and water vapor condenses/freezes on nuclei.
  • Important lifting mechanisms: convection, frontal lifting, orographic/terrain lifting, and convergence.
  • Knowing the lifting mechanism helps predict where cloud, turbulence, precipitation, and icing may occur.

Stability

  • Stable air resists vertical displacement: commonly smoother flight, layered/stratiform clouds, haze/fog, and steady precipitation.
  • Unstable air supports vertical motion: commonly turbulence, cumulus/cumulonimbus development, showery precipitation, and better visibility between clouds.
  • Whether a lifted parcel continues rising depends on the environmental lapse rate, parcel moisture, and adiabatic cooling/warming.

Fronts

  • A front is a boundary/transition zone between air masses with differing properties.
  • Cold fronts often move faster and can force warm air upward abruptly, producing a narrower band of convective/showery weather.
  • Warm fronts often have gentler overrunning and widespread layered cloud/precipitation ahead of the surface front.
  • Stationary and occluded fronts can also produce extensive cloud/precipitation. Actual weather varies with moisture, stability, wind, terrain, and front strength—do not use a cartoon front as a forecast.

Fog / visibility

  • Fog is a surface-based cloud and forms by different mechanisms such as radiation cooling, advection of moist air over a colder surface, upslope lift, or evaporation/steam processes.
  • Temperature–dew-point convergence, light winds, clear nights, moist ground, and terrain can all be clues depending on fog type.
  • Visibility can also be reduced by haze, smoke, precipitation, dust, mist, and low cloud—not only fog.

Use weather theory as a trend detector

  • A METAR is a snapshot. Weather theory helps ask what happens next: Is moisture increasing? Is a front approaching? Is daytime heating destabilizing the air? Are winds strengthening with a tightening pressure gradient?
  • Preflight and in-flight decisions should use observations + forecasts + trends + PIREPs/advisories, not a single report.

What to remember

  • Weather = unequal heating + pressure + moisture + stability + lift.
  • Small T–Td spread = closer to saturation.
  • Close isobars = stronger pressure gradient.
  • Stable: layered/smooth; unstable: vertical/cumulus/turbulent (general patterns, not guarantees).
  • Know lifting mechanisms and front signatures.
  • Translate theory into hazards and trends.
