Lesson 49 — How to Fly Cross Country

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • This lesson demonstrates using a paper VFR chart and nav log during an actual cross-country.
  • The nav log gives expected heading, checkpoint times, groundspeed, and fuel. In flight, compare actual vs. planned and update the plan.
  • The operating cycle is: fly accurately, identify position, note time, evaluate error, correct early.

Before takeoff

  • Complete weather, NOTAM, fuel, performance, route, airspace, and aircraft-preflight work before relying on the nav log.
  • If using a VFR flight plan, file it before departure and remember that filing is not the same as opening it.
  • Write down actual takeoff time and start a timer or clock for navigation.
  • The transcript distinguishes the approximate departure time used for Flight Service from the more precise times useful for checkpoint navigation.

Departure and first leg

  • After takeoff, establish the planned climb speed and heading while prioritizing aircraft control, traffic, and terrain.
  • Avoid becoming heads-down with paperwork during the high-workload departure phase.
  • The instructor uses top of climb as an early point where groundspeed and timing estimates become more reliable than during the changing-speed climb.

Checkpoint technique

  • Choose checkpoints that are visually distinctive and easy to identify from the air.
  • At each checkpoint, note actual crossing time and compare it with planned time.
  • A consistent early/late trend indicates actual groundspeed differs from forecast. Update the remaining ETA rather than carrying the original error through the flight.
  • Use outside references, chart features, and heading/time together; one uncertain visual feature is weak position evidence.

Heading and track corrections

  • Heading is where the nose points; track is the path over the ground. Wind can make them different.
  • If left or right of course, make a deliberate correction and reassess rather than chasing every small deviation.
  • Cross-check the compass/heading system and account for wind changes shown by actual progress.
  • If the planned heading produces the desired track, maintain it even when the nose does not point directly at the destination.

Time, groundspeed, and ETA

  • Once distance between known checkpoints and elapsed time are known, estimate actual groundspeed.
  • Use actual groundspeed to recalculate time remaining: time = distance ÷ groundspeed.
  • The instructor emphasizes keeping navigation times close—roughly to the minute when practical—because small timing errors compound across legs.

Fuel monitoring

  • Update fuel estimates when actual flight time or fuel flow differs from the plan.
  • Do not wait until late in the flight to discover that reserve margin has eroded.
  • Fuel decisions should include expected burn to destination plus regulatory reserve and a practical buffer for uncertainty.

Radio and flight-plan workflow

  • Make normal departure and traffic calls appropriate to the airport and airspace.
  • When using a VFR flight plan, ensure it is opened after departure and closed after arrival; it primarily supports search-and-rescue services and is not an ATC clearance.
  • Flight following is a separate ATC service. It can add traffic advisories and assistance when available, while the VFR pilot still retains see-and-avoid responsibility.

If the plan stops matching reality

  • Maintain aircraft control, then determine whether the mismatch comes from wind, heading, timing, position identification, weather, or a route change.
  • If unsure of position, use all available resources—chart, GPS/navaids, prominent features, ATC, or Flight Service—rather than continuing on a guess.
  • Divert early when fuel, weather, daylight, terrain, passenger condition, or aircraft performance begins consuming the planned margin.

Technique vs. rule

  • The instructor’s exact clock tolerances, headings, radio calls, and checkpoint choices are demonstration techniques for that flight, not universal requirements.
  • For actual operations, follow current regulations, published procedures, the aircraft POH/AFM, and your instructor’s methods.

What to remember

  • Plan → fly → identify → time → compare → correct.
  • Record actual takeoff and checkpoint times.
  • Use checkpoint timing to update groundspeed, ETA, and fuel.
  • Correct track errors early and deliberately.
  • VFR flight plan and flight following are different services.
  • Change the plan while plenty of options remain.
