Lesson 42 — PIREPs

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • A PIREP is a pilot report of actual conditions encountered aloft.
  • PIREP value is highest where surface observations/forecasts cannot tell the whole story: cloud tops/bases, turbulence, icing, visibility between stations, wind shear, and conditions over terrain.
  • One report is a point in space/time from a particular aircraft—not a guarantee that the same intensity exists everywhere nearby.

Why PIREPs matter

  • They let pilots compare forecast conditions with what aircraft are actually encountering.
  • Forecasters/controllers also use them to improve hazard awareness and forecasts/advisories.
  • A report that “weather doesn't match the forecast” can be operationally important even when the condition is not severe.

Where to find them

  • Current aviation-weather products can display PIREPs graphically and/or as coded text; third-party EFBs may aggregate the same data.
  • Use map symbols as a quick filter, then read the actual report text, time, altitude, location, and aircraft type.
  • Website interfaces change; know the product rather than memorizing the video's old menu path.

PIREP fields

  • /OV location/over; /TM time UTC; /FL altitude/flight level; /TP aircraft type.
  • /SK sky/cloud layers; /WX weather/visibility; /TA temperature; /WV wind.
  • /TB turbulence; /IC icing; /RM remarks.
  • Not every field appears in every PIREP; interpret only what is actually reported.

Reading location/time/altitude

  • Location may be a navaid/fix/airport reference with direction and distance. Plot it relative to your route instead of assuming “nearby” means relevant.
  • Time is UTC/Zulu; compare report age with weather movement.
  • Reported altitude is normally MSL/flight-level context. Conditions at 8,000 ft do not automatically describe 3,000 ft.

Aircraft type changes interpretation

  • Turbulence and icing intensity are partly based on effect on the reporting aircraft.
  • “Moderate turbulence” from a large transport and from a light trainer can represent different physical/effect contexts; always note /TP.
  • Likewise, an aircraft's anti-ice/deice equipment and speed can affect icing experience.

Turbulence

  • Reports can describe intensity such as light, moderate, severe, or extreme, duration/frequency, and sometimes altitude range.
  • Do not infer that a single smooth-air PIREP means the route is smooth; turbulence can be localized and change rapidly.
  • Mountain-wave, convective, frontal, and clear-air turbulence may exist with very different spatial patterns.

Icing

  • PIREP icing information can include intensity/type and altitude range.
  • Icing reports are particularly valuable because temperature/moisture forecasts indicate potential, while PIREPs confirm actual encounters.
  • A no-icing report does not prove no icing exists nearby; cloud liquid-water content varies.

Routine vs urgent

  • Routine PIREPs are commonly labeled UA.
  • UUA urgent PIREPs are for especially hazardous conditions under reporting criteria, including severe/extreme turbulence, severe icing, low-level wind shear, tornado/funnel cloud/waterspout, volcanic ash, and other serious hazards.
  • Treat an urgent report as a major planning signal; assess movement, route, altitude, and escape options rather than simply noting the icon.

Giving a PIREP

  • Provide useful reports through ATC/FSS or approved electronic means when workload permits.
  • Include precise position, time, altitude, aircraft type, and the relevant weather condition/intensity.
  • If ATC requests a PIREP, report what you actually observe; “negative icing” or “tops 6,500” can be valuable too.

Don't confuse colored map symbols with the report

  • The transcript groups graphical symbols into turbulence, icing, and other reports. Those icons are a visualization layer.
  • Operational interpretation comes from the underlying coded/decoded PIREP and its context, not color alone.

What to remember

  • PIREP = actual airborne condition.
  • Always read location, time, altitude, and aircraft type.
  • Especially valuable for icing, turbulence, and cloud tops/bases.
  • UA routine; UUA urgent.
  • One PIREP is a data point, not a blanket forecast.
  • Give useful PIREPs when conditions merit it.
