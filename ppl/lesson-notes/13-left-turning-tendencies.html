Lesson 13 — Left Turning Tendencies

Core idea

  • Single-engine propeller airplanes commonly experience several effects that tend to yaw/roll left, especially at high power and low airspeed.
  • The four commonly taught effects are torque, P-factor, spiraling slipstream, and gyroscopic precession.

Torque

  • Newton’s third law: if the engine/propeller turns one way, the airframe tends to roll the opposite way.
  • In many U.S. trainers the propeller turns clockwise as seen from the cockpit, creating a left-rolling tendency.
  • Torque effect is strongest with high power.

P-factor

  • At high AOA, the descending propeller blade has a larger effective AOA/relative airflow than the ascending blade.
  • The descending blade produces more thrust, shifting the thrust center and yawing the airplane left.
  • P-factor is most noticeable at high power, high AOA, and low airspeed.

Spiraling slipstream

  • Propeller wash forms a corkscrew around the fuselage.
  • It can strike the left side of the vertical tail and push the tail right, yawing the nose left.
  • The effect is prominent at high power and lower forward speed.

Gyroscopic precession

  • A spinning propeller behaves as a gyroscope.
  • A force applied to the spinning disk produces a reaction approximately 90° later in the direction of rotation.
  • This is especially relevant in tailwheel airplanes during rapid pitch changes; magnitude/direction depends on prop rotation and pitch change.

Pilot response

  • Use the rudder required to maintain coordinated flight and desired heading—often right rudder in high-power climb.
  • Do not memorize a fixed amount of rudder; use outside references and the coordination indicator.
  • Effects change with power, airspeed, AOA, aircraft design, and phase of flight.

What to remember

  • Torque, P-factor, spiraling slipstream, gyroscopic precession.
  • Most noticeable high power / low speed / high AOA.
  • Maintain coordination with appropriate rudder.
