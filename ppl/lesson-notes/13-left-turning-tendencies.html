Lesson 13 — Left Turning Tendencies

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
In many single-engine trainers with a clockwise-turning propeller as seen from the cockpit, several propeller/engine effects combine to create left roll/yaw tendencies. They are strongest in the high-power, low-airspeed, high-AOA conditions common during takeoff and climb.

Core idea

  • The four commonly taught left-turning effects are torque reaction, P-factor, spiraling slipstream, and gyroscopic precession.
  • They do not all have the same strength in every condition; each depends differently on power, airspeed, AOA, pitch changes, propeller rotation, and airplane design.
  • The practical pilot response is not to memorize a fixed rudder amount: use appropriate rudder pressure to maintain heading/coordination, often right rudder during high-power climb in common U.S. trainers.

1. Torque reaction

  • Newton's third law provides the basic idea: the engine/propeller rotating one way applies a reaction torque to the airframe in the opposite direction.
  • For the common trainer example with a propeller rotating clockwise as viewed from the cockpit, the reaction tends to roll the airplane left.
  • Torque reaction is strongest when the engine is producing high torque/power and becomes more noticeable when aerodynamic roll resistance/control effectiveness is lower, such as at low airspeed.
  • The transcript notes that manufacturers can design in compensations — such as engine/fin/wing geometry or trim — so the airplane is better balanced in a typical cruise condition.
  • Those design compromises cannot perfectly cancel the effect at every speed and power setting, which is why the pilot still needs rudder/control input outside the design point.

2. P-factor / asymmetric propeller loading

  • A propeller blade is an airfoil producing thrust rather than wing lift.
  • When the propeller disk is inclined relative to the incoming airflow — especially at high AOA — the descending and ascending blades experience different effective angles/relative velocities.
  • In the common clockwise-prop example, the descending blade on the right side produces more thrust than the ascending blade on the left.
  • The resultant thrust is therefore offset to the right side of the propeller disk, creating a yawing moment that turns the nose left.
  • P-factor is most noticeable with high power, high AOA, and relatively low airspeed.

3. Spiraling slipstream

  • The propeller accelerates air rearward, and that slipstream follows a rotating/corkscrew path around the fuselage.
  • On the common trainer arrangement, the spiraling flow strikes the left side of the vertical stabilizer/rudder area.
  • Pushing the tail to the right yaws the nose left.
  • The effect is especially noticeable when propeller flow is strong compared with the airplane's forward airflow: high power and lower airspeed.
  • As forward airspeed increases, the spiral is effectively stretched out and the tail's ordinary relative airflow becomes more dominant.

4. Gyroscopic precession

  • A spinning propeller has angular momentum and behaves like a gyroscope.
  • When the orientation of the propeller disk is changed, the gyroscopic reaction appears approximately 90° later in the direction of rotation.
  • This effect is most important when the airplane is changing pitch rapidly while the propeller has substantial RPM.
  • It is particularly noticeable in tailwheel airplanes when the tail is raised during the takeoff roll because the propeller disk is being pitched into a new plane.
  • The direction of the resulting yaw depends on propeller rotation and the direction of the pitch change; “gyroscopic precession always turns left” is not a universal statement.

Why takeoff/climb demands right rudder

  • Takeoff and initial climb combine several conditions that strengthen the left-turning tendencies: high power, low-to-moderate airspeed, and relatively high AOA.
  • The airplane may therefore yaw/roll left if the pilot simply applies full power and does nothing else.
  • Use rudder as needed to keep the nose/flight path aligned and the coordination indicator centered.
  • As airspeed, power, and pitch change, the required rudder pressure changes too. Continually adjust rather than holding a memorized pedal position.

Design compensation does not eliminate pilot responsibility

  • The instructor discusses airplanes being rigged/designed so asymmetric tendencies are partially canceled at a typical cruise speed.
  • Possible methods include offset engine thrust lines, aerodynamic offsets, trim tabs, or other asymmetry in the design/rigging.
  • The consequence of optimizing one flight condition is that some residual tendency remains at other speeds/power settings.
  • This is another reason the pilot should fly the coordination cues, not assume the airplane is automatically neutral.

Keep the four effects distinct

  • Torque is primarily an opposite roll reaction to engine/prop rotation.
  • P-factor is asymmetric thrust across the propeller disk at inclined/high-AOA operation.
  • Spiraling slipstream is rotating propwash striking the tail asymmetrically.
  • Gyroscopic precession comes from changing the plane of a spinning propeller disk.
  • All four can influence yaw/roll, but they are different physical mechanisms and should not be collapsed into a generic “prop makes airplane go left.”

What to remember for review

  • Torque, P-factor, spiraling slipstream, gyroscopic precession.
  • High power / low airspeed / high AOA is the classic condition where the combined left-turning tendency is strongest.
  • Torque: opposite roll reaction.
  • P-factor: descending blade produces more thrust → left yaw in the common trainer example.
  • Spiraling slipstream: propwash strikes left side of tail → tail right / nose left.
  • Gyroscopic precession: response 90° later in rotation; most noticeable with rapid prop-disk pitch changes.
  • Maintain desired heading and coordination with the rudder required for the actual condition.
