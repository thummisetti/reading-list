Lesson 33 — The Magnetic Compass

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The magnetic compass aligns with Earth's magnetic field and supplies a simple heading reference independent of the aircraft's vacuum system and, in a conventional installation, electrical system.
  • The lesson stresses two practical reasons to understand it: conventional heading indicators need periodic compass alignment, and the compass remains an important backup if electronic heading information fails.
  • Its usefulness comes with several predictable errors that are common written/oral-exam material.

How it works / why it looks backwards

  • Magnetized elements align the compass card with the Earth's magnetic field while the airplane rotates around it.
  • Because the pilot views the inside/opposite side of the compass card, numbers appear to move opposite the direction of a turn. This is one reason the heading indicator is easier to use for maneuvering.
  • The compass is normally filled with damping fluid to steady oscillations and lubricate/support the assembly.

Magnetic dip

  • Earth's magnetic field has a vertical component; compass magnets tend to dip toward the Earth, especially at higher magnetic latitudes.
  • Compass design uses balancing/weighting to keep the card approximately level, but that compensation creates the classic acceleration and turning errors in the Northern Hemisphere.

Variation and deviation

  • Variation is the angular difference between true north and magnetic north at a geographic location. Isogonic lines on aeronautical charts show variation.
  • Deviation is error caused by magnetic/electrical influences within the aircraft itself.
  • A compass swing minimizes deviation; remaining error is listed on the compass correction card.
  • Variation is geographic; deviation is airplane-specific. Do not confuse them.

Turning error — UNOS

  • In the Northern Hemisphere, use UNOS: Undershoot North, Overshoot South when rolling out of turns using only the magnetic compass.
  • On northerly headings the compass lags the turn, so lead/undershoot the indicated target.
  • On southerly headings the compass leads, so continue/overshoot the indicated target before rollout.
  • Turning error is greatest near north/south and minimal near east/west.

Acceleration error — ANDS

  • On east/west headings in the Northern Hemisphere: ANDS = Accelerate North, Decelerate South.
  • Acceleration can make the compass momentarily indicate a turn toward north; deceleration can make it indicate toward south even if heading has not changed.
  • This error is most noticeable on east/west headings and is tied to magnetic dip/compass weighting.

Oscillation and lag

  • Turbulence and maneuvering can make the card swing/oscillate, reducing precision.
  • Whenever possible, set/check heading in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight after the compass has settled.

Using it with the heading indicator

  • A conventional unslaved heading indicator drifts, so compare it to the magnetic compass periodically—commonly about every 15 minutes in training practice.
  • Do not reset the HI while turning or accelerating because compass error may be large at that moment.

What to remember

  • Compass = required/basic magnetic direction reference and valuable backup.
  • Variation = true vs magnetic; deviation = aircraft-induced error.
  • Northern Hemisphere: UNOS for turns and ANDS for acceleration/deceleration.
  • Read/set it in straight, unaccelerated flight when possible.
  • Use the compass correction card and current chart variation.
