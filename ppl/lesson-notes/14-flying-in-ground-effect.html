Lesson 14 — Flying in Ground Effect

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
This lesson is really two connected topics: wingtip vortices / wake turbulence, and how flying near the surface interferes with those airflow patterns to create ground effect.

Core idea

  • Wingtip vortices are a byproduct of producing lift. Pressure differences around the wing drive spanwise flow around the tips, forming rotating vortices and contributing to induced drag.
  • Stronger lift demand / higher AOA generally produces stronger vortices and more induced drag.
  • Ground effect occurs when the airplane flies close enough to the ground or water that the surface interferes with normal wingtip-vortex and downwash development.
  • The result is less induced drag and greater aerodynamic efficiency than the same airplane would have out of ground effect.
  • That changes both takeoff and landing behavior: the airplane may lift off early and accelerate in ground effect, or float farther during landing.

How wingtip vortices form

  • A lifting wing has lower pressure over much of the upper surface and higher pressure beneath it.
  • Near each wingtip, higher-pressure air from below tends to flow outward and curl upward toward the lower-pressure region above the wing.
  • That spanwise/upward flow combines with the wing's downwash and rolls into a rotating vortex trailing behind each wingtip.
  • The vortices are part of the three-dimensional flow field that creates induced drag.
  • At lower airspeed, the airplane generally needs a higher lift coefficient/AOA to support the same weight, which strengthens the vortex system and induced drag.

Wake turbulence hazard

  • The rotating air behind another airplane is wake turbulence.
  • A small airplane encountering the strong wake of a larger aircraft can experience a roll rate beyond its available aileron authority, especially close to the ground.
  • Takeoff and landing are particularly critical because airplanes are close to the surface, relatively slow, and have little altitude for recovery.
  • Wake strength is greatest when the generating airplane is heavy, clean, and slow; high lift demand creates strong vortices.
  • Avoid flying directly through or below/behind a larger aircraft's vortex path. Apply current ATC wake-separation procedures and pilot wake-avoidance techniques rather than relying on a single memorized distance.

What ground effect changes

  • Close to a surface, the ground interferes with the spanwise flow, vortex growth, and downwash around the wing.
  • Wingtip vortices become weaker and downwash is reduced.
  • The reduction in downwash reduces the wing's induced angle of attack and therefore induced drag.
  • For a given geometric AOA and airspeed, the wing operates more efficiently; equivalently, the airplane can produce the required lift with less induced drag / less required thrust.
  • The transcript phrases this as “less induced drag and more lift.” A more precise operational interpretation is improved lift-to-drag performance near the surface, not free extra lift independent of pitch/airspeed.

How close is “ground effect”?

  • The existing notes' useful rule of thumb remains: ground effect becomes noticeable within roughly one wingspan of the surface and becomes increasingly pronounced closer to the surface, especially around half a wingspan or less.
  • The strength depends on actual wing height above the surface, which is why the transcript notes that low-wing airplanes generally experience a stronger effect at a given wheel height than high-wing airplanes.
  • Ground effect works over water as well as land because the key mechanism is interference from a nearby surface, not soil specifically.

Landing implications — float

  • As the airplane enters ground effect during the flare, induced drag decreases.
  • Because less energy is being dissipated by induced drag, the airplane can float farther before touching down.
  • Excess approach speed makes the float much more pronounced and lengthens landing distance.
  • On a long training runway that may merely be inconvenient; on a short runway it can erase stopping margin.
  • The lesson's practical message is to fly the recommended approach/landing speed rather than carrying unnecessary extra speed into ground effect.

Takeoff implications — premature liftoff

  • Because the airplane needs less induced drag/thrust to remain airborne in ground effect, it may lift off before it has enough speed or performance to climb safely out of ground effect.
  • If the pilot tries to climb away immediately, the airplane loses the induced-drag benefit as it rises. Required drag/thrust increases and climb performance may collapse.
  • The transcript describes possible consequences as reduced lift margin, increased induced drag, loss of airspeed, and reduced controllability/stability margin.
  • The safest conceptual rule: do not interpret “the wheels came off the runway” as proof that the airplane has adequate climb performance.
  • Use POH/AFM takeoff speeds and technique, especially with high density altitude, heavy weight, runway contamination/softness, or obstacles.

Accuracy note — airspeed indication in ground effect

  • The transcript suggests ground effect can make the airspeed indicator read faster because of a “pressure change.”
  • Ground proximity can influence pitot/static position error on some airplanes, but there is no universal rule that ground effect always makes IAS read high.
  • Do not compensate by inventing an airspeed correction. Fly the aircraft's published speeds and use the POH/AFM for any known airspeed-system errors.

Soft-field takeoff — deliberate use of ground effect

  • A soft-field takeoff is the important exception discussed in the lesson where the pilot may intentionally become airborne at a relatively low speed to remove wheel drag from a soft surface.
  • After liftoff, the airplane is deliberately held in ground effect while it accelerates.
  • The critical point from the transcript: do not climb out of ground effect until adequate climb speed is achieved.
  • The exact pitch attitude, flap setting, liftoff technique, and climb speed are aircraft- and procedure-specific; use the POH/AFM and flight instruction.

What to remember for review

  • Wingtip vortices come from the pressure/flow field of a lifting wing and contribute to induced drag.
  • Higher lift demand / high AOA generally means stronger vortices.
  • Wake turbulence from a heavy, clean, slow airplane can be hazardous, especially to a smaller airplane close to the ground.
  • Ground effect = surface interference with wing airflow → weaker vortices/downwash → less induced drag.
  • It is strongest very close to the surface and generally affects a low wing more strongly at the same wheel height.
  • Landing: excess speed + reduced induced drag → longer float.
  • Takeoff: airplane can lift off in ground effect before it can climb out of it.
  • Soft-field takeoff intentionally uses ground effect to accelerate before climbing away.
  • Use published speeds/technique; ground effect does not create extra engine power.
