Lesson 9 — What Makes an Airplane Turn?

Comprehensive condensed study notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training.

Low-bandwidth review notes: preserve the lesson’s core instructional material, definitions, relationships, and practical implications while removing repetition and filler.

Core idea

  • An airplane turns because bank tilts the lift vector.
  • The tilted lift vector has a horizontal component that accelerates the airplane toward the center of the turn.

Lift vector in a turn

  • In straight-and-level flight, lift is primarily vertical and equals weight.
  • Banking tilts total lift; part remains vertical and part becomes horizontal.
  • The horizontal component provides the centripetal force that changes direction.
  • To maintain altitude in a level turn, the pilot must increase total lift because only part of it is now vertical.

Bank, load, and stall

  • Increasing bank angle in a level turn increases required lift and therefore load factor.
  • Higher load factor increases stall speed.
  • A steep, slow, level turn can therefore reach critical AOA at an indicated airspeed that would not stall the airplane in 1-G flight.

Turn radius and rate

  • More bank generally increases rate of turn and decreases turn radius at a given airspeed.
  • Higher airspeed increases turn radius for a given bank angle.
  • A coordinated turn balances bank and yaw so occupants do not feel strong sideways acceleration.

Coordination

  • Ailerons establish bank; rudder counters adverse yaw and keeps the turn coordinated.
  • Too little/too much rudder creates a slip or skid.
  • Skidding turns near the stall are particularly hazardous because they can promote spin entry.

What to remember

  • Bank does not 'make the nose turn' by itself; it tilts lift.
  • Horizontal lift component turns the airplane.
  • Maintain altitude → increase total lift → higher load factor/stall speed.
  • Coordinate with rudder.
