Lesson 9 — What Makes an Airplane Turn?

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Ailerons roll the airplane, but the turn itself comes from the lift vector being tilted. The lesson builds the chain from aileron deflection → bank → horizontal lift component → curved flight path → increased load factor in a level turn.

Core idea

  • An airplane turns because banking tilts the total lift vector.
  • The tilted lift vector can be resolved into a vertical component and a horizontal component.
  • The horizontal component accelerates the airplane toward the center of the turn — the centripetal force needed to change direction.
  • Ailerons establish the bank; rudder coordinates the yaw. Neither “steers” the airplane like a car's front wheels.

How the ailerons start the maneuver

  • Ailerons move in opposite directions.
  • Lowering an aileron increases local wing camber and lift; raising the opposite aileron decreases local lift.
  • The lift difference produces a rolling moment, banking the airplane.
  • The instructor offers the memory aid “thumbs up, ailerons up”: when the yoke is rotated toward one side, the thumb on that side points up and that side's aileron goes up.
  • Ailerons primarily create roll. The bank they establish is what reorients lift so the airplane can turn.

Why bank alone is not the whole explanation

  • The instructor explicitly asks why a banked airplane does not simply continue straight ahead while tilted.
  • The answer is that the aerodynamic lift force rotates with the banked airplane.
  • Once lift is tilted, part of it acts sideways rather than entirely upward.
  • That sideways/horizontal component continuously changes the airplane's velocity direction, producing the curved flight path.

Lift-vector components

  • In straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight, the lift vector is approximately vertical and its magnitude balances weight.
  • In a bank, total lift is tilted. Its vertical component is what opposes weight; its horizontal component is what turns the airplane.
  • If the pilot banks without increasing total lift, the vertical component becomes smaller than weight and the airplane tends to descend.
  • To maintain altitude in a level turn, the pilot must increase total lift, normally by increasing AOA with back pressure and often adjusting power as required.
  • More required total lift means the wing and airplane carry a higher aerodynamic load.

Coordination and adverse yaw

  • The aileron that goes down produces extra lift and also extra drag, creating adverse yaw.
  • Rudder is used with aileron to oppose that unwanted yaw and keep the airplane coordinated while rolling into and out of the turn.
  • A coordinated turn keeps the airplane aligned appropriately with its curved flight path rather than slipping or skidding.
  • Too little or too much rudder creates a slip or skid; a skidding turn near a stall is especially dangerous because stall + yaw can lead to spin entry.

Load factor appears as bank increases

  • Because a level turn requires more total lift than straight-and-level flight, the airplane experiences a higher load factor.
  • The transcript introduces load factor as the increased apparent aerodynamic/inertial load associated with the turn and deliberately defers the detailed treatment to the next lessons.
  • As bank angle becomes steep in a level coordinated turn, required load factor rises nonlinearly.
  • The instructor uses the word “exponentially”; the precise relationship for an ideal level coordinated turn is load factor = 1 / cos(bank angle), which rises very rapidly as bank approaches 90° but is not an exponential function.
  • More load factor means more lift must be produced and therefore a higher AOA is required for a given airspeed.
  • This increases the airplane's stall speed: it can reach critical AOA at a higher indicated airspeed than in 1-G flight.
  • Extreme load factor can also exceed structural limits.

Bank angle, rate, radius, and airspeed

  • For a given airspeed, increasing bank angle generally increases turn rate and decreases turn radius.
  • For a given bank angle, increasing airspeed increases turn radius and reduces angular turn rate.
  • Those relationships assume a coordinated turn and comparable conditions; wind changes the ground track but not the basic air-mass turn mechanics.

Worth watching — lift-vector diagram

  • The key visual in this lesson is the total lift vector tilting with bank and being split into vertical and horizontal components.
  • If you remember only one image from the video, make it this one: vertical lift supports; horizontal lift turns.
  • The aileron animation is also useful for reinforcing differential lift and the “thumbs up, ailerons up” memory aid.

What to remember for review

  • Ailerons create roll; bank tilts the lift vector.
  • Horizontal lift component = turning force.
  • Vertical lift component = weight-supporting force.
  • To hold altitude in a bank, increase total lift.
  • More bank in a level turn → higher required load factor → higher stall speed.
  • Use rudder with aileron to counter adverse yaw and maintain coordination.
  • Steep, slow, uncoordinated turns are dangerous because they combine high AOA/load with yaw.
