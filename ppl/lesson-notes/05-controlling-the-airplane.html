Lesson 5 — Controlling the Airplane

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
The goal of this page is to preserve the complete instructional model: three axes, the primary controls, how each surface creates a moment, and why aileron and rudder must be coordinated.

Core idea

  • An airplane can rotate in three dimensions about three axes that intersect near its center of gravity (CG).
  • The three basic rotational motions are pitch, roll, and yaw.
  • The primary flight controls change aerodynamic forces on the airplane and create moments about those axes.
  • The written-exam mapping to memorize is: lateral axis → pitch → elevator; longitudinal axis → roll → ailerons; vertical axis → yaw → rudder.
  • In normal maneuvering the controls work together, especially aileron and rudder, rather than as three completely independent systems.

Center of gravity and why the airplane rotates

  • The lesson revisits the center of gravity as the average point at which the airplane's weight is concentrated.
  • The instructor uses the familiar balance/string analogy: if a model airplane were suspended at its CG, it would balance around that point.
  • Moving weight forward moves the CG forward; moving weight aft moves the CG aft.
  • In flight, the airplane's aerodynamic forces and weight act at different locations and create moments around the CG.
  • The existing notes' useful mechanics remain: moment = force × moment arm. A larger force or a longer distance from the pivot creates a larger turning effect.
  • This is why relatively modest forces at the tail can strongly affect pitch: the tail is a long distance from the CG.

Terminology note — “center of lift”

  • The instructor uses center of lift for the theoretical point where the wing's total lift can be treated as acting.
  • FAA/aerodynamics references more commonly use center of pressure for the point where the resultant aerodynamic force is considered concentrated; its location can move with AOA.
  • The lesson's practical point is still useful: the CG is normally arranged relative to the aerodynamic forces so the airplane has a predictable pitching balance, and the tail supplies the remaining balancing moment.

The three axes

  • Lateral axis: runs wingtip to wingtip. Rotation about it is pitch — nose up or nose down.
  • Longitudinal axis: runs nose to tail. Rotation about it is roll — one wing up, the other down.
  • Vertical axis: runs vertically through the airplane. Rotation about it is yaw — nose left or right.
  • The instructor notes a common naming trap: the airplane's longitudinal motion/shape is perpendicular to the lateral axis about which it pitches. The axis is named for its orientation, not for the direction the nose appears to move.

Pitch — elevator and the lateral axis

  • The elevator is the primary control for pitch.
  • The pilot controls it with the yoke or control stick.
  • Pulling aft on a conventional yoke/stick raises the elevator. This changes aerodynamic force on the horizontal tail, driving the tail downward and rotating the nose upward about the CG/lateral axis.
  • Pushing forward lowers the elevator, changes the tail force in the opposite direction, and pitches the nose downward.
  • A pitch change alters AOA and therefore can affect lift, induced drag, airspeed, climb/descent, and stall margin.
  • Some airplanes use a stabilator (an all-moving horizontal tail) rather than a separate fixed stabilizer/elevator, but the control function is still pitch.

Roll — ailerons and the longitudinal axis

  • Ailerons, near the outboard trailing edges of the wings, control roll.
  • They move in opposite directions when the pilot rotates the yoke or moves the stick laterally.
  • The down-going aileron increases local camber and lift on that wing; the up-going aileron decreases local lift on the other wing.
  • The lift difference creates a rolling moment about the longitudinal axis.
  • To initiate a left bank, the left aileron moves up and the right aileron moves down; for a right bank the opposite occurs.
  • The ailerons primarily bank the airplane. The bank then tilts the lift vector, creating the horizontal force component that actually curves the flight path; the detailed turn physics comes in Lesson 9.

Adverse yaw — why rolling also yaws

  • The down-going aileron not only produces more lift; it also produces more drag.
  • That extra drag pulls the wing with the lowered aileron rearward, tending to yaw the nose opposite the desired direction of roll. This is adverse yaw.
  • Example: to roll left, the right aileron goes down. The right wing's added drag tends to yaw the nose right while the airplane is trying to bank left.
  • This is why the lesson insists that aileron use be accompanied by appropriate rudder input.

Yaw — rudder and the vertical axis

  • The rudder is the primary control for yaw around the vertical axis.
  • It is controlled by the rudder pedals.
  • Pressing the right rudder pedal deflects the rudder so the airplane yaws right; pressing left rudder yaws left.
  • The rudder's central role in normal maneuvering is directional control and coordination, especially correcting adverse yaw.
  • Rudder is also important for crosswind control and for countering propeller-related yawing tendencies discussed later in the course.
  • Rudder alone is not the normal mechanism for making a coordinated turn; bank/tilted lift is what produces the turn.

Coordinating aileron and rudder

  • When rolling left, use left aileron with an appropriate amount of left rudder to oppose adverse yaw.
  • When rolling right, use right aileron with appropriate right rudder.
  • Coordination is also required when rolling out of a turn. For example, rolling out of a left turn requires right aileron and typically corresponding right rudder.
  • The lesson uses the inclinometer/“ball” as the feedback cue: if the ball remains centered, the maneuver is coordinated.
  • If the ball is displaced, use the familiar cue “step on the ball” to apply rudder toward the displaced ball and improve coordination.
  • The exact rudder pressure is not a fixed amount; use enough to keep the airplane coordinated as power, speed, bank, and configuration change.

Control effectiveness depends on airflow

  • Ailerons, elevator, and rudder are aerodynamic surfaces, so their effectiveness depends on airflow over them.
  • At higher airspeed, a given control deflection generally produces a stronger aerodynamic force.
  • At low airspeed, larger deflections may be needed for the same rotational response.
  • Propeller slipstream can increase airflow over parts of the tail in many single-engine airplanes, which is one reason power changes can affect control feel and yaw/pitch response.
  • Near a stall, disturbed/separated airflow can reduce control effectiveness and make coordination even more important.

What to remember for review

  • Pitch: lateral axis, elevator/stabilator, yoke/stick fore-aft.
  • Roll: longitudinal axis, ailerons, yoke/stick left-right.
  • Yaw: vertical axis, rudder, pedals.
  • The airplane rotates about axes through/near the CG because aerodynamic forces create moments.
  • Ailerons move oppositely and create differential lift.
  • The down-going aileron also creates extra drag → adverse yaw.
  • Use coordinated rudder with aileron both entering and exiting turns.
  • Centered ball = coordinated; displaced ball = correct with appropriate rudder.
  • Control authority depends on airflow over the control surfaces.
