Lesson 5 — Controlling the Airplane

Core idea

  • The airplane rotates about three axes: longitudinal (roll), lateral (pitch), and vertical (yaw).
  • Primary flight controls change airflow and aerodynamic forces to rotate the airplane about those axes.
  • Control effectiveness depends strongly on airflow over the control surfaces.

Three axes and primary controls

  • Ailerons control roll about the longitudinal axis.
  • Elevator/stabilator controls pitch about the lateral axis.
  • Rudder controls yaw about the vertical axis.
  • The three controls are coordinated in normal maneuvering rather than used as isolated systems.

Ailerons

  • Ailerons move in opposite directions.
  • The down-going aileron increases local camber/lift on one wing; the up-going aileron decreases it on the other, producing roll.
  • The wing producing more lift also tends to produce more induced drag, contributing to adverse yaw.
  • Rudder is used to keep turns coordinated.

Elevator and pitch

  • Moving the elevator changes the aerodynamic force on the horizontal tail.
  • That tail-force change creates a pitching moment about the CG.
  • Pitch attitude changes angle of attack and therefore affects lift, drag, airspeed, climb/descent, and stall margin.

Rudder and yaw

  • Rudder changes side force on the vertical tail, yawing the nose left or right.
  • Rudder is especially important for coordination, crosswind control, and countering propeller-related yawing tendencies.
  • Rudder alone is not the normal way to turn an airplane; bank creates the horizontal lift component that turns it.

Moment and moment arm

  • A moment is a turning effect: force × distance from the pivot/reference point.
  • A longer moment arm means a smaller force can create the same turning effect.
  • Aircraft controls and CG location matter because they change these moments.

What to remember

  • Roll—ailerons; pitch—elevator; yaw—rudder.
  • Controls work by changing aerodynamic force and moments.
  • Coordinate aileron and rudder to avoid slips/skids and adverse yaw.
