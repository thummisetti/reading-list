Lesson 29 — The Attitude Indicator

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The attitude indicator, or artificial horizon, displays aircraft pitch and bank relative to the horizon.
  • Traditional units are gyroscopic instruments; modern glass panels normally derive the same information from AHRS.
  • It gives attitude—not altitude, airspeed, vertical speed, or actual flight path by itself.

Reading the display

  • The miniature airplane represents the aircraft and the colored horizon/sky-ground display represents the horizon.
  • The bank pointer/index shows roll angle. The lesson walks through typical marks at 10°, 20°, 30°, 45°, and 60°, although exact markings vary by instrument.
  • Pitch-reference marks show nose-up/nose-down attitude; many instruments allow the miniature airplane/reference symbol to be adjusted to the pilot's normal sightline.
  • With the roll pointer centered, bank is 0°. The miniature wings relative to the horizon line make the direction and approximate magnitude of bank visually obvious.

How the mechanical instrument works

  • A spinning gyro uses rigidity in space to maintain a reference while the airplane pitches and rolls around the instrument.
  • In many legacy trainers the attitude gyro is vacuum-driven; other installations can be electric.
  • Because the attitude indicator and heading indicator may share a vacuum source, one vacuum-system failure can compromise both.

Pitch/bank limits and tumbling

  • Older mechanical attitude indicators have design limits. Extreme pitch or bank can cause the gyro/gimbals to reach their travel limits, tumble, or give unreliable indications.
  • After unusual attitudes or a power/vacuum interruption, do not assume the indication is immediately valid; allow the installed instrument to recover/erect as specified.
  • Electronic AHRS systems fail differently—usually through flags, red Xs, comparator messages, or reversionary modes—so know the actual panel.

Cross-check and interpretation

  • Use the attitude indicator as part of an instrument cross-check with airspeed, altimeter, VSI, heading/turn information, and—when VMC—the outside horizon.
  • If the AI says the airplane is climbing but altitude/VSI/airspeed trends disagree, investigate rather than blindly following one instrument.
  • A display can be smooth and believable while wrong; redundancy and cross-check are what reveal a failed source.

Practical human factors

  • In VFR flight, the outside view remains primary for collision avoidance and attitude awareness; do not become heads-down simply because the instrument is easy to read.
  • In inadvertent IMC, a functioning attitude reference is extremely valuable, but safe recovery also depends on instrument proficiency and avoiding conflicting sensory cues.

What to remember

  • AI/artificial horizon = pitch + bank reference.
  • Bank marks commonly include 10/20/30/45/60°, but check the actual instrument.
  • Mechanical units use a gyro and may have tumbling/operating limits.
  • Know the power source and failure indications.
  • Cross-check; never trust one display in isolation.
