Lesson 12 — V-Speeds Explained

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
The lesson uses V-speeds to connect aerodynamics with the airspeed indicator. The important hierarchy is: know what the indicator is actually showing, know the color arcs, know the common V-speed meanings, and use the actual airplane's POH/AFM values.

Core idea

  • V-speeds are standardized labels for important aircraft operating/performance speeds.
  • The letter/symbol meaning is standardized, but the numeric value is aircraft- and often condition-specific.
  • The airspeed-indicator arcs package several important speeds into an immediately usable cockpit display.
  • V-speeds matter aerodynamically because aircraft handling, control effectiveness, stall margin, structural margin, climb performance, and flap limitations all depend on airspeed.

Knots and nautical miles

  • A knot is one nautical mile per hour.
  • The instructor uses a simple example: 60 kt means traveling through 60 nautical miles of air in one hour, if maintained.
  • Do not confuse knots with statute miles per hour; aviation commonly uses nautical miles and knots.

Indicated, calibrated, true, and groundspeed

  • Indicated airspeed (IAS): the airspeed read directly from the airspeed indicator, uncorrected for instrument/position error.
  • Calibrated airspeed (CAS): IAS corrected for instrument and position error. The POH/AFM commonly provides a conversion table.
  • True airspeed (TAS): actual speed of the airplane relative to the surrounding air mass, after correcting for density effects.
  • Groundspeed (GS): speed of the airplane over the ground. Wind changes groundspeed relative to TAS.
  • Example from the transcript: 60 kt TAS with a 10-kt direct headwind gives about 50 kt groundspeed; with a 10-kt direct tailwind, about 70 kt groundspeed.
  • Groundspeed is what determines how long it takes to cover a route over Earth; airspeed is what the wing/control surfaces “care about” aerodynamically.

Accuracy notes on the transcript's airspeed explanation

  • The video at one point says a steady headwind directly “increases” the wind entering the pitot tube and a tailwind decreases it. The cleaner model is: the ASI senses the airplane's motion relative to the air mass. If you maintain the same TAS/IAS, a steady headwind changes groundspeed, not indicated airspeed by itself.
  • The video also says true airspeed “does not change with altitude.” More precisely: for a given IAS/CAS, TAS generally increases as density decreases with altitude. A particular TAS can of course be held at multiple altitudes if power/attitude permit.
  • The ASI is not simply “wrong on purpose.” It displays indicated airspeed based on pitot-static pressure; differences from CAS/TAS arise from position/instrument error and air-density/compressibility effects.
  • The transcript correctly emphasizes that the POH provides corrections and that IAS is deliberately useful because many aerodynamic limits and handling references are published as indicated airspeeds.

Pitot-static connection

  • The airspeed indicator compares pitot total/ram pressure with static pressure.
  • The difference is dynamic pressure, which is related to airflow speed and air density.
  • Position/installation effects, especially at high AOA and unusual configurations, can make IAS differ from CAS.
  • The detailed pitot-static instrument mechanics are covered later in the course; here the purpose is to understand why the indicated speed is the practical aerodynamic reference used with the colored arcs.

Airspeed-indicator color arcs

  • White arc — flap operating range: lower limit is VSO; upper limit is VFE.
  • Green arc — normal operating range: lower limit is VS1; upper limit is VNO.
  • Yellow arc — caution range: operation only in smooth air and with caution.
  • Red radial line — VNE: never-exceed speed.
  • The arcs are airplane-specific. Do not transfer the numerical markings from one airplane to another.

Stall and flap V-speeds

  • VSO: stall speed/minimum steady flight speed in the landing configuration (commonly full landing flap and gear as applicable). It forms the lower end of the white arc.
  • VS1: stall speed/minimum steady flight speed in a specified configuration; commonly associated with the clean configuration and the lower end of the green arc on simple trainers.
  • VFE: maximum flap-extended speed / upper end of the white arc. Exceeding it with flaps extended can overstress the flap system/airframe.

Structural-speed markings

  • VNO: maximum structural cruising speed / top of the green arc. Above VNO is the yellow caution range.
  • VNE: never-exceed speed / red line. It is a hard operating limitation, not a target to approach.
  • The yellow arc is not a normal cruise range; it is reserved for smooth-air operation with caution because gust/turbulence loads can be hazardous at high speed.

VA — maneuvering speed

  • VA is design maneuvering speed and is tied to aerodynamic/structural load limits.
  • VA varies with aircraft weight; lighter weight → lower VA.
  • Below the applicable VA, under the certification concept, a single abrupt full control input is expected to stall the wing before exceeding the positive limit load.
  • VA is not a magic turbulence-proof speed and does not protect against every gust, repeated control reversal, or combined multi-axis input.
  • Use the POH/AFM's VA for the current weight or its approved method of adjustment.

VY — best rate of climb

  • VY gives the greatest altitude gain per unit of time under the specified conditions.
  • The instructor's memory aid is essentially “get high with VY.”
  • VY is normally used when you want to gain altitude efficiently in time after obstacle considerations are satisfied.
  • The transcript connects VY to the airplane's drag/power relationship and notes it lies in a relatively efficient speed region.

VX — best angle of climb

  • VX gives the greatest altitude gain per unit of horizontal distance.
  • It is used for obstacle clearance when the POH procedure calls for it.
  • VX is normally slower than VY at ordinary operating altitudes.
  • The distinction: VY gets you highest soonest; VX gets you highest over the shortest ground/air distance.
  • In a normally aspirated propeller airplane, indicated VX generally rises and indicated VY generally falls with altitude until they converge near the absolute ceiling.

Best glide

  • Best-glide speed is the aircraft-specific speed used to maximize glide performance after power loss (subject to the POH's definition/conditions).
  • It is associated with an efficient lift-to-drag condition but the exact published speed can vary with weight/configuration.
  • Use the POH/AFM value rather than assuming it equals another V-speed or L/D-max speed numerically in every airplane.

Worth watching — airspeed concepts and color arcs

  • The IAS/CAS/TAS/groundspeed diagrams are worth seeing once because the “speed through air” versus “speed over ground” distinction is easier visually.
  • The color-coded airspeed indicator is the most efficient way to memorize VSO/VFE/VS1/VNO/VNE relationships.
  • The VX-versus-VY obstacle illustration is also worth watching if “angle” versus “rate” still feels abstract.

What to remember for review

  • IAS = what the indicator shows; CAS = IAS corrected for instrument/position error; TAS = speed relative to air mass corrected for density; GS = speed over ground.
  • Wind primarily changes groundspeed for a maintained airspeed.
  • White arc: VSO to VFE.
  • Green arc: VS1 to VNO.
  • Yellow arc: caution / smooth air.
  • Red line: VNE.
  • VSO, VS1, VFE, VNO, VNE, VA, VX, VY are core written-test V-speeds.
  • VX = altitude per distance; VY = altitude per time.
  • VA varies with weight.
  • All numerical V-speeds come from the actual airplane's POH/AFM and markings.
