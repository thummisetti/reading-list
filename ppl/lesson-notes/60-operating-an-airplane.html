Lesson 60 — Operating an Airplane

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • Operating an airplane safely requires more than manipulating the controls. A pilot should understand the aircraft well enough to preflight it, start and run it correctly, recognize abnormal indications, and use the POH/checklist intelligently.
  • The video teaches common light-piston systems. Exact fuel, electrical, induction, ignition, mixture, cooling, and abnormal procedures are aircraft-specific; the actual POH/AFM is authoritative.

Preflight inspection

  • The lesson starts with a simple point: inspect the entire aircraft before flight because many problems are much harder to discover or address after takeoff.
  • Use the manufacturer's checklist rather than relying only on memory or a habitual walkaround route.
  • Look for structural damage, leaks, loose/missing hardware, tire/brake condition, flight-control freedom/security, fuel/oil quantity and contamination, pitot/static openings, antennas/lights, and anything inconsistent with normal condition.
  • A physical preflight complements—but does not replace—the maintenance/inspection-status review from the previous lesson.

Reciprocating four-stroke engine

  • Intake: intake valve opens and the descending piston draws the fuel/air charge into the cylinder.
  • Compression: valves close and the rising piston compresses the mixture.
  • Power: spark ignites the compressed charge and expanding gases drive the piston downward.
  • Exhaust: exhaust valve opens and the rising piston pushes combustion products out.
  • Proper power requires the correct fuel/air mixture, compression, ignition timing, cooling, and lubrication.

Ignition and magnetos

  • Typical training-airplane piston engines use two magnetos and two spark plugs per cylinder for redundancy and more complete/consistent combustion.
  • Magnetos are engine-driven self-contained ignition sources, so ignition does not depend on the normal alternator/battery bus once the engine is running.
  • A magneto check during run-up verifies each ignition system can operate independently within POH limits.
  • Because a broken/open P-lead can leave a magneto “hot,” treat the propeller as potentially capable of firing the engine whenever it is moved.

Detonation vs. preignition

  • Detonation is abnormal explosive combustion after normal ignition begins, producing excessive pressure/temperature and potentially damaging the engine.
  • Contributors can include excessive cylinder temperature, too-low fuel octane, excessive manifold pressure/high power for conditions, or an inappropriate mixture setting.
  • If detonation is suspected, follow the POH: typical helpful actions may include reducing power, enriching mixture where appropriate, and increasing cooling airflow.
  • Preignition occurs when the charge ignites before the normal spark event, often from an overheated hot spot such as a damaged plug/deposit. It can create very high temperatures/pressures and serious damage.
  • Do not try to diagnose severe engine roughness solely from a textbook distinction in flight; use the abnormal checklist, reduce stress on the engine as appropriate, and plan to land if the engine's condition is doubtful.

Fuel system

  • Fuel may reach the engine by gravity feed, engine-driven/electric pumps, or a combination depending on design.
  • Know usable vs. total fuel, tank-selector positions, venting, sump/drain locations, fuel-pump procedures, and the indications available in the actual airplane.
  • Visually confirm fuel quantity when practical and sample drains for water, sediment, and correct fuel grade/color as applicable.
  • A fuel gauge reading is only one input; compare expected burn, time, known quantity, and tank management throughout flight.

Carburetor and induction icing

  • A carburetor's venturi pressure drop plus fuel vaporization can lower temperature enough for ice to form even when outside air is well above freezing.
  • Carb ice is favored by moisture/high relative humidity and can occur at surprisingly warm temperatures.
  • In a fixed-pitch airplane, developing carb ice typically appears as an unexplained RPM decrease; in a constant-speed installation, manifold-pressure indications may be more useful.
  • Carb heat introduces warmer alternate/unfiltered air, normally causing an immediate power decrease. If ice was present, power may initially worsen as it melts, then recover.
  • Fuel-injected engines do not have carburetor ice in the carburetor sense, but induction-system icing/blockage remains possible.

Mixture control

  • The correct fuel/air mixture changes with air density, power setting, and engine design. As altitude increases, a mixture left full rich can become excessively rich.
  • Leaning can restore efficient combustion, reduce plug fouling, and improve performance at altitude when done per POH procedure.
  • At high-elevation airports, POH procedures may call for leaning even during ground operation/takeoff preparation.
  • Too-rich operation can reduce power and foul plugs; an improperly lean high-power setting can create excessive temperatures in some engines/settings.
  • On descent into denser air, enrich progressively as the POH directs rather than blindly using one universal “full rich” rule; high-density-altitude airports may require a leaned takeoff/go-around mixture.

Oil and cooling

  • Engine oil lubricates moving parts, carries heat away, helps clean/suspend contaminants, seals between parts, and protects against corrosion.
  • Monitor oil pressure and temperature trends. Low oil pressure, high oil temperature, or a worsening combination can indicate a serious lubrication problem.
  • Air-cooled engines depend on airflow through cowling/baffles and proper operating technique; prolonged high-power/low-airspeed operation can increase temperature.

Electrical system

  • The alternator/generator normally powers the electrical system and recharges the battery; the battery provides starting power and temporary backup energy.
  • An alternator failure generally does not stop a magneto-ignition piston engine, but the battery will eventually discharge and electrically powered avionics/lights/equipment will be lost.
  • Recognize low-voltage/ammeter/loadmeter indications, verify the failure per checklist, and shed nonessential loads as directed.
  • Bus architecture, circuit breakers/fuses, standby batteries, essential buses, and reset policies are airplane-specific.

Engine indications as a system

  • Do not stare at one gauge in isolation. Oil pressure/temp, cylinder-head temperature, EGT, RPM, manifold pressure, fuel flow/pressure, voltage/current, and engine sound/roughness together tell a more complete story.
  • Trend changes matter: a slowly deteriorating indication can provide time to reduce load, troubleshoot with the checklist, and land before it becomes a major failure.

Technique caveat

  • The transcript includes specific mixture, detonation, and preignition techniques. Those illustrate the concepts; the POH/AFM and engine-manufacturer guidance for the actual installation control.
  • Do not transfer a Cessna-style procedure to a different fuel, turbocharging, FADEC, constant-speed-propeller, or engine installation without checking its approved procedure.

What to remember

  • Preflight with the aircraft checklist and investigate anything abnormal before launch.
  • Four strokes: intake, compression, power, exhaust.
  • Magnetos are independent of the normal electrical bus.
  • Carb ice can occur in warm, humid air.
  • Mixture must match density, power, and POH procedure.
  • Detonation and preignition are damaging abnormal-combustion conditions—cool/reduce engine stress as directed and treat persistent abnormal indications seriously.
  • An alternator failure can leave the engine running while electrical capability decays.
  • Know the actual airplane's fuel, ignition, electrical, oil/cooling, and abnormal-checklist architecture.
