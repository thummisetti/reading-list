Lesson 56 — Safety Regulations

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • This lesson is a rapid tour of Part 91 and NTSB rules that frequently appear in private-pilot study: required equipment, restraints, overwater equipment, alcohol/drugs, operating conduct, speed limits, oxygen, ELTs, and occurrence reporting.
  • Regulatory minimums are the legal floor. Personal operating limits should often be more conservative.

Required equipment — §91.205

  • §91.205 is a key reference for powered civil aircraft with standard U.S. airworthiness certificates.
  • For day VFR, memory aids such as ATOMATOFLAMES can help recall common items, but the regulation—not the mnemonic—is authoritative.
  • Night VFR adds lighting/electrical equipment; IFR adds further instruments/equipment.
  • Equipment required by type design, a KOEL, operating limitations, ADs, or another rule may apply even when it is absent from a memorized §91.205 list.

Seat belts and shoulder harnesses

  • The PIC must ensure required passenger notification/briefing on how to fasten and unfasten restraints.
  • Occupants generally use approved seats or berths with seat belts secured during surface movement, takeoff, and landing, subject to the regulation's stated exceptions.
  • Required crewmembers keep seat belts fastened while at their stations; shoulder-harness use applies as specified, with the stated exception when necessary to perform required duties.
  • The practical lesson is to use properly installed restraint equipment as intended rather than treating it as an inconvenience.

Overwater equipment

  • The transcript highlights additional flotation and signaling requirements for certain for-hire operations beyond power-off gliding distance from shore.
  • Even where a particular rule does not require it for a personal flight, suitable flotation and signaling equipment can be prudent risk management for significant overwater exposure.

Alcohol, drugs, and fitness — §91.17

  • No person may act or attempt to act as a crewmember within 8 hours after consuming alcohol, while under the influence, while using a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety, or at/above the regulation's alcohol-concentration threshold.
  • “8 hours bottle to throttle” is only a minimum rule, not proof of fitness. Residual effects, fatigue, dehydration, illness, and medications can still make flight unsafe.
  • Self-assessment remains essential even when the numerical rule has been met.

Careless/reckless operation and dropping objects

  • Part 91 prohibits careless or reckless operation so as to endanger the life or property of another.
  • Dropping an object is not categorically prohibited; it may not create a hazard to persons or property.
  • A narrow permission under one rule never removes the broader responsibility to operate safely.

Speed limits — §91.117

  • The broad limit is no more than 250 KIAS below 10,000 ft MSL, unless an applicable exception or authorization applies.
  • Lower limits apply in specified terminal-area airspace near Class B/C/D airports and beneath or through Class B areas as provided by the rule.
  • Aircraft limitations, turbulence guidance, traffic, or conditions may require a substantially lower speed than the regulatory ceiling.

Supplemental oxygen — §91.211

  • At cabin pressure altitudes above 12,500 ft MSL through 14,000 ft MSL, required flightcrew use supplemental oxygen for the portion of flight exceeding 30 minutes.
  • Above 14,000 ft MSL, required flightcrew use oxygen continuously.
  • Above 15,000 ft MSL, each occupant must be provided supplemental oxygen.
  • These are legal thresholds; individual physiology and night-vision considerations can justify oxygen at lower altitudes.

Emergency locator transmitter (ELT)

  • Most applicable U.S.-registered civil airplanes require an approved, attached, operable ELT unless an exception applies.
  • The installation receives the required periodic inspection, including installation, battery condition, controls/crash sensor, and signal checks.
  • Battery replacement or recharge is based on the applicable use/life triggers; verify the actual aircraft maintenance records rather than relying on memory.
  • After an operation in which inadvertent activation is plausible, verify ELT status and follow the appropriate reset/reporting procedure.

NTSB Part 830

  • The transcript distinguishes FAA operating rules from NTSB notification and reporting rules.
  • Part 830 contains a specific list of aviation occurrences requiring immediate NTSB notification. The lesson's important study point is to know where that list lives and consult the current text rather than guessing.
  • Part 830 also addresses preservation of the aircraft, wreckage, cargo, and records after a reportable occurrence, with stated exceptions for safety and protection.
  • Written-report requirements and timing depend on the event and NTSB direction; use the current Part 830 text for exact applicability.

Study strategy

  • Know the high-frequency numbers—8 hours, 250 below 10,000, and the 12,500/14,000/15,000 oxygen thresholds—but also know which regulation each number belongs to.
  • For equipment questions, use §91.205 together with type design/KOEL/operating limitations and the inoperative-equipment framework rather than one mnemonic alone.

What to remember

  • §91.205 = key required-equipment reference.
  • Restraints: brief passengers and use required belts/harnesses.
  • Alcohol: 8 hours is a legal minimum; be fully fit.
  • 250 KIAS below 10,000 MSL is the broad speed ceiling, with lower terminal-area limits.
  • Oxygen: above 12,500 for more than 30 min; above 14,000 crew use; above 15,000 provide to occupants.
  • Know ELT inspection and battery status.
  • NTSB Part 830 governs occurrence notification and preservation/reporting requirements.
