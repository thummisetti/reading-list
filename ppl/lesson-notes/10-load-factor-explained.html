Lesson 10 — Load Factor Explained

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
Load factor connects aerodynamics, maneuvering, stall speed, and structural limits. The two major hazards are exceeding critical AOA at an unexpectedly high airspeed and exceeding the airplane's allowable structural load.

Core idea

  • Load factor is the ratio of the aerodynamic/supporting load acting on the airplane to its weight, conventionally expressed in Gs.
  • In ordinary straight-and-level unaccelerated flight, load factor is approximately 1 G.
  • At 2 G, the airplane and occupants experience an apparent/supporting load about twice their ordinary weight; at 4 G, about four times.
  • Load factor can rise in turns, pull-ups, abrupt maneuvering, and turbulence — a turn is not required.
  • Higher load factor means both greater structural stress and higher stall speed.

Why a level turn increases load factor

  • In straight-and-level flight, total lift is approximately vertical and equal to weight.
  • Banking tilts total lift. Only its vertical component supports the airplane against weight; the horizontal component turns the airplane.
  • If the pilot wants to maintain altitude, total lift must increase until its vertical component again balances weight.
  • That increased total lift is the higher load carried by the wings.
  • As bank angle increases in a coordinated level turn, required load factor follows n = 1 / cos(bank angle).
  • At 60° of bank, load factor is approximately 2 G.
  • As bank approaches 90°, the theoretical level-turn load factor rises without bound; a true level 90°-bank turn cannot be sustained by ordinary wing lift alone.

G as a way to express apparent load

  • The instructor uses body-weight examples to make G intuitive: a 200-lb person at 1 G experiences roughly a 200-lb support load; at 2 G, roughly 400 lb; at 4 G, roughly 800 lb.
  • Likewise, the transcript uses a roughly 2,300-lb C172 at 4 G to illustrate a wing load on the order of 9,200 lb in the simplified sense of four times the airplane's weight.
  • These are intuition-building examples: load factor is a ratio, not the airplane literally gaining mass.

Load factor without a turn

  • The instructor gives a pull-out example: descend and then abruptly pitch up/level off.
  • The airplane's inertia resists the rapid change in flight path, so the wing must create a larger aerodynamic force to curve the path upward.
  • A faster entry speed and/or more abrupt pull can produce a larger load factor.
  • Turbulence can do something similar by rapidly changing AOA and aerodynamic force without the pilot intentionally commanding a steep turn.

Accelerated stalls

  • To produce more lift at the same airspeed, the wing must operate at a higher lift coefficient, commonly by increasing AOA.
  • Because critical AOA does not change simply because the airplane is pulling more G, a higher load factor causes the wing to reach critical AOA at a higher indicated airspeed.
  • This is an accelerated stall: the airplane stalls above its ordinary 1-G stall speed because the required lift/load is higher.
  • The standard relationship is VS,new = VS,1G × √n.
  • At 2 G, stall speed is about 1.414 times the 1-G stall speed.
  • This is why a steep, level turn or hard pull can stall an airplane even when the airspeed indicator is well above the published 1-G stall speed.

Structural limits

  • Aircraft have approved positive and negative load-factor limits that vary by aircraft, certification category, weight/configuration, and sometimes flap position.
  • The actual limits for the airplane are found in the POH/AFM and placards/limitations.
  • The transcript uses a C172S POH example with normal and utility categories to show that allowable loads can differ with operating category/weight.
  • Do not treat a published limit as a target. Repeated or severe loading can damage the airframe even if a catastrophic wing separation does not occur.
  • Structural overload can create permanent deformation or hidden damage that requires inspection before further flight.

Accuracy notes on the transcript's structural analogies

  • The video says retracted flaps make the wing structurally “stronger.” A better interpretation is that approved load-factor limits are often lower with flaps extended because the airplane is in a different configuration with flap-system/aerodynamic limits; flap retraction does not simply turn the wing structure itself into stronger metal.
  • The paper-clip analogy is useful for understanding fatigue, but the instructor's speculation about a particular accident/gender-reveal event is not evidence about that accident's actual cause and is omitted as a factual conclusion.
  • Aircraft age alone does not mean “metal gets weaker” in a simple linear way. Fatigue life depends on stress cycles, material, environment, corrosion, damage history, inspections, repairs, and design.

Positive and negative G

  • Positive G is the familiar load pressing the occupant into the seat and loading the wing in its usual positive-lift direction.
  • Negative G occurs when acceleration/support force is in the opposite sense, making occupants feel lifted toward the restraint rather than pressed into the seat.
  • Many normal-category airplanes tolerate substantially less negative G than positive G; the exact limits are aircraft-specific.
  • The transcript's phrase that “weight produces an upward force” during negative G is not the best physics description: gravity still acts downward; what reverses is the direction of the net/supporting acceleration and apparent load.

Maneuvering speed connection

  • The existing notes' point remains important: maneuvering speed (VA) is related to the intersection of aerodynamic stall and structural-load limits for the specified condition/weight.
  • Below the applicable VA, an abrupt full control input in one axis is intended to make the wing stall before the airplane exceeds its positive limit load under the certification assumptions.
  • VA does not make the airplane immune to all structural damage, multiple simultaneous control inputs, repeated reversals, severe turbulence, or operation outside limitations.
  • VA changes with weight; use aircraft data rather than assuming a single number always applies.

  • The person/airplane “effective weight” examples make 2 G and 4 G easy to visualize, as long as you remember mass has not actually changed.

What to remember for review

  • Load factor = aerodynamic/support load ÷ weight.
  • Straight-level unaccelerated flight ≈ 1 G.
  • Steeper level bank → more required lift → higher G.
  • 60° level bank ≈ 2 G.
  • Pull-ups and turbulence can produce high load factor without a turn.
  • Higher G → higher stall speed: stall speed varies with √load factor.
  • Higher G also increases structural stress.
  • Positive and negative load limits differ; configuration/category can change limits.
  • Use POH/AFM limits; never treat the limit as a normal operating target.
