Lesson 10 — Load Factor Explained

Core idea

  • Load factor is aerodynamic load divided by aircraft weight and is expressed in Gs.
  • 1 G means the airplane/occupants are supporting a load equal to their weight.

Load factor in turns

  • In a level banked turn, lift must increase to keep the vertical component equal to weight.
  • As bank angle increases, required total lift—and therefore load factor—increases.
  • At 60° of bank in a coordinated level turn, load factor is about 2 G.
  • Load factor rises very rapidly as bank approaches 90°, making altitude-holding at extreme bank impractical.

Why load factor matters

  • Higher load factor increases structural stress.
  • Higher load factor increases the AOA/lift demand for a given airspeed and therefore increases stall speed.
  • Maneuvering, turbulence, abrupt control inputs, and steep turns can all change G-load.

Stall speed relationship

  • Accelerated stalls occur when critical AOA is reached while load factor is greater than 1 G.
  • Approximate relationship: stall speed varies with the square root of load factor.
  • Example: at 2 G, stall speed is roughly 1.414 times the 1-G stall speed.

Aircraft limits

  • Aircraft are certificated for positive/negative load-factor limits by category.
  • Exceeding structural limits can permanently deform or fail the airframe.
  • Published maneuvering speed helps protect against full-control-input structural overload in the specified sense, but it does not make the airplane immune to all damage or turbulence.

What to remember

  • Load factor = lift/load ÷ weight.
  • Steeper level bank → higher G.
  • Higher G → higher stall speed and structural load.
  • 60° level bank ≈ 2 G.
