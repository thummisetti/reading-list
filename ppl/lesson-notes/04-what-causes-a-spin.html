Lesson 4 — What Causes a Spin?

Comprehensive condensed notes for Josh Campbell / Free Pilot Training — merged from the full lesson transcript and the existing study notes.

▶ Watch lessonBack to course
These notes preserve the lesson's substantive instruction and test-oriented discussion while removing repeated caption fragments and filler. Spin recovery is aircraft-specific: use your POH/AFM and qualified flight instruction, not a generic mnemonic alone.

Core idea

  • A spin is an aggravated stall with yaw that produces autorotation and a descending, corkscrew-like flight path.
  • A stall by itself is not enough to create a developed spin. The airplane must also have yaw / sideslip asymmetry at or beyond the stall.
  • Spin prevention is therefore mainly about two habits: avoid exceeding critical AOA and keep the airplane coordinated, especially when slow or close to the ground.
  • If a spin begins, the underlying stall must be broken and the yaw/rotation stopped using the aircraft-specific recovery procedure.

What makes a spin different from a stall

  • The lesson starts from the stall concept in Lesson 3: at least part of the wing has exceeded critical AOA.
  • For a spin, the airplane is also yawed so the two wings are not experiencing identical local airflow and AOA.
  • One wing becomes more deeply stalled than the other and produces less lift and more drag.
  • The more-stalled wing drops and the drag asymmetry reinforces yaw, creating autorotation.
  • Once established, the airplane descends while rotating around a near-vertical axis / corkscrew path.
  • Low-altitude spins are especially hazardous because meaningful altitude is required to recognize the condition, stop the rotation, break the stall, and recover from the resulting descent.

Coordinated versus uncoordinated flight

  • In coordinated flight, yaw is properly matched to the airplane's bank/turn so there is no excessive lateral slip or skid.
  • If the tail is not aligned appropriately with the airplane's path, the airplane is in an uncoordinated condition.
  • A slip or skid describes different types of uncoordinated flight, depending on the relationship between bank, yaw, and flight path.
  • The lesson uses the inclinometer — commonly called “the ball” — as the cockpit cue for coordination.
  • With a conventional slip/skid ball, the familiar correction is “step on the ball”: use rudder pressure on the side toward which the ball is displaced to restore coordination.
  • The instructor repeatedly stresses using rudder with aileron during normal turns so adverse yaw does not leave the airplane uncoordinated.

Adverse yaw matters near a stall

  • Yaw does not have to come only from an intentional rudder input. The lesson explicitly points out adverse yaw from aileron deflection.
  • When one aileron is lowered to increase lift on that wing, it also increases drag on that wing, tending to yaw the airplane opposite the intended roll direction.
  • Normally, coordinated rudder input counters that adverse yaw.
  • Near a stall, an uncorrected yaw asymmetry is more serious because the wings are already close to critical AOA; one can become more deeply stalled and start autorotation.
  • This is why a pilot should not think “I only need to worry about rudder-caused yaw.” Any significant yaw/sideslip at the stall can contribute.

The classic base-to-final danger

  • The practical scenario to fear is a slow, high-AOA, uncoordinated turn close to the ground.
  • A common example is overshooting the final approach course and trying to force the airplane back toward the runway with excessive inside rudder while holding bank with opposite aileron — a skid.
  • The skid creates yaw while the airplane may already be slow and at a high AOA.
  • If critical AOA is then exceeded, the combination of stall + yaw can produce a rapid spin entry with too little altitude for recovery.
  • The better prevention is to go around rather than salvage a badly overshot, uncoordinated base-to-final turn.

Incipient versus developed spin

  • The lesson distinguishes the incipient phase — the beginning of spin entry — from a fully developed spin.
  • Early in the entry, promptly reducing AOA and correcting the yaw may stop the event before a stable spin develops.
  • The instructor's practical message is: recognize and break the stall early rather than waiting for multiple rotations.
  • The transcript includes the instructor's personal “nine times out of ten” characterization of early recovery. Treat that as anecdotal emphasis, not a performance guarantee.

Spin prevention

  • Do not exceed critical AOA, especially at low altitude.
  • Maintain coordinated flight; use the inclinometer and appropriate rudder.
  • Correct adverse yaw during turns rather than letting the tail swing out of alignment.
  • Be especially disciplined during slow flight, traffic-pattern turns, approach, and go-around.
  • If you are approaching a stall, reduce AOA promptly before the stall can combine with yaw.
  • Do not intentionally enter a spin unless the aircraft is approved for it, all limitations are satisfied, sufficient altitude exists, and the training is conducted appropriately.

Generic spin-recovery concept

  • The lesson discusses the common training mnemonic PARE as a generic way to remember the major recovery ideas.
  • P — Power: reduce to idle as specified by the recovery procedure.
  • A — Ailerons: neutral. Aileron deflection in a stalled condition can worsen the asymmetry or delay recovery.
  • R — Rudder: apply full rudder opposite the direction of rotation as specified.
  • E — Elevator: move sufficiently forward to reduce AOA / break the stall as specified by the airplane's procedure.
  • When rotation stops, neutralize the rudder and recover smoothly from the resulting dive, observing airspeed and structural limits.
  • The POH/AFM overrides the mnemonic. Some airplanes use different procedures, and some prohibit intentional spins entirely.

Accuracy note — don't turn the transcript's spoken sequence into a checklist

  • The instructor informally describes “breaking the stall first” and then gives a spoken PARE-like sequence in which some elevator wording is ambiguous.
  • Do not memorize that conversational wording as a universal sequence. For an actual airplane, the manufacturer's approved spin-recovery procedure is the controlling reference.
  • The durable aerodynamic ideas are: remove the stall, stop the yaw/autorotation, then recover from the descent without overstress.

Why aileron alone is not the answer

  • Trying to “pick up the low wing” with aileron while the airplane is stalled can increase the asymmetry between the wings.
  • A lowered aileron can increase local camber/AOA and drag on one wing, potentially deepening its stalled condition.
  • That is why the generic spin-recovery concept calls for neutral ailerons while the yaw is stopped with rudder and the stall is broken with elevator/AOA reduction.

Wing twist / washout discussion

  • The lesson notes that many training-airplane wings incorporate geometric twist so the wing root tends to reach critical AOA before the tip.
  • This design helps preserve aileron effectiveness longer as the wing approaches a stall.
  • It does not make the airplane “spin-proof.” A sufficiently stalled and yawed airplane can still enter a spin.

Test-question nuance discussed in the lesson

  • The instructor raises a possible written-test question: if an airplane spins to the right, which wing or wings are stalled?
  • He notes that the Airplane Flying Handbook wording allows a spin to develop when at least one wing is stalled while yaw/sideslip exists.
  • He nevertheless says the test-oriented “most correct” answer in the scenario is often both wings are stalled, with one wing more deeply stalled than the other.
  • Do not infer spin direction merely from “which wing stalled first.” The rotation direction is tied to the yaw/drag/lift asymmetry during the event.

Spin versus spiral dive

  • A spin and a spiral dive can both involve rapid altitude loss and rotation, but they are aerodynamically different.
  • In a spin, the wing is stalled and recovery requires breaking the stall and stopping autorotation.
  • In a spiral dive, the wing is generally not stalled; airspeed and load factor can build rapidly as the turn tightens.
  • Because the conditions differ, the recovery technique differs. Correctly identify the condition rather than applying a memorized response blindly.

What to remember for review

  • Spin = stall + yaw/sideslip.
  • The wings are aerodynamically asymmetric; one is more deeply stalled and autorotation develops.
  • Maintain coordination, especially when slow/high-AOA and in the traffic pattern.
  • Adverse yaw can provide the yaw component even without an intentional bad rudder input.
  • Never try to salvage a dangerous skidding base-to-final turn; go around.
  • Early recognition matters: reduce AOA and correct yaw before a developed spin forms.
  • Generic PARE concept: power idle, ailerons neutral, opposite rudder, reduce AOA.
  • After rotation stops, neutralize rudder and recover smoothly from the dive.
  • Always use the actual airplane's POH/AFM recovery procedure and limitations.
