Lesson 55 — Avoiding a Crash

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The lesson combines the regulatory right-of-way rules with practical see-and-avoid technique. Knowing who legally has priority is useful, but collision avoidance always comes first.
  • Never insist on right-of-way if doing so creates a collision. Predictable, early avoidance is safer than waiting for the other aircraft to react.

See-and-avoid responsibility

  • When weather conditions permit, pilots are responsible for maintaining vigilance to see and avoid other aircraft, even when receiving ATC services.
  • Traffic advisories, ADS-B traffic, TCAS-like displays, and flight following are aids—not substitutes for an outside scan.
  • Clear before turns, climbs, descents, practice maneuvers, and runway/pattern entries.

How to scan

  • Use short, deliberate visual sectors and allow the eyes time to focus rather than making one fast continuous sweep.
  • Look around windshield posts, wings, cabin structure, and other blind spots. Move your head when necessary.
  • Spend extra attention near airports, VORs/checkpoints, practice areas, and other places where aircraft tend to converge.
  • A target that remains at nearly the same relative bearing while growing larger/closer is a classic collision cue: constant bearing + decreasing range.

Aircraft in distress

  • An aircraft in distress has right-of-way over all other air traffic.
  • Give it room and avoid creating additional conflict while it deals with the emergency.

Converging aircraft

  • When aircraft of the same category are converging at approximately the same altitude, the aircraft to the other's right has right-of-way.
  • The aircraft that does not have right-of-way should alter course to pass well clear.
  • Category hierarchy also matters: less-maneuverable aircraft receive priority in the order specified by §91.113, and aircraft towing/refueling other aircraft have special priority over engine-driven aircraft.

Head-on

  • When aircraft approach each other head-on or nearly so and there is danger of collision, each alters course to the right.
  • Both pilots making the same predictable maneuver prevents the ambiguity that occurs if one turns left and one turns right.

Overtaking

  • The aircraft being overtaken has right-of-way.
  • The overtaking aircraft must alter course to the right and pass well clear.

Landing

  • Aircraft on final approach to land or landing have right-of-way over other aircraft in flight or operating on the surface, subject to the rule's details.
  • When two or more aircraft are approaching to land, the lower aircraft generally has right-of-way—but it may not exploit that rule by cutting in front of or overtaking another aircraft already on final.
  • Right-of-way does not justify an unstable approach or forcing a conflict; go around when spacing is unsafe.

Minimum safe altitude as collision/forced-landing margin

  • Except when necessary for takeoff or landing, fly high enough that a power-unit failure would allow an emergency landing without undue hazard to persons or property on the surface.
  • Over a congested area, the familiar §91.119 rule is at least 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 2,000 ft horizontally.
  • Over other than congested areas, generally remain at least 500 ft above the surface; over open water or sparsely populated areas, remain at least 500 ft from any person, vessel, vehicle, or structure.
  • These are regulatory minimums, not recommended cruise altitudes for every situation; terrain, glide options, noise, traffic, and personal margins can call for more altitude.

Lights and conspicuity

  • Use required position and anti-collision lights under the applicable rules and aircraft limitations.
  • Lights can improve detection in haze, twilight, and busy airspace, but they do not make an aircraft reliably visible.
  • Sun angle, haze, dirty windshields, cockpit fixation, and background clutter can make traffic very difficult to see.

Practical hierarchy

  • Aviate and avoid first; sort out who had legal priority second.
  • Make collision-avoidance changes early enough to be obvious, and continue visually monitoring the other aircraft until safely clear.

What to remember

  • See-and-avoid remains your responsibility even with ATC/traffic displays.
  • Distress aircraft: priority.
  • Head-on: both right.
  • Overtaking: pass on the right; overtaken aircraft has priority.
  • Converging: aircraft on the right generally has priority when otherwise comparable.
  • Landing: lower/final aircraft has priority, but may not cut in.
  • Constant bearing + decreasing range = collision threat.
  • Know the §91.119 minimum-altitude framework and use greater margin when prudent.
