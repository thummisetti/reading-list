Lesson 32 — The Altimeter

▶ Watch lesson   Back to course

Core idea

  • The altimeter is essentially an aneroid barometer calibrated in feet. It uses static pressure to estimate altitude.
  • Because atmospheric pressure changes with weather as well as height, the pilot must set the current pressure reference and understand pressure/temperature error.
  • The lesson emphasizes altimeter knowledge as likely oral/written-exam material.

Five altitude terms

  • Indicated altitude: what the altimeter reads with the current setting in its barometric window.
  • True altitude: actual height above mean sea level (MSL).
  • Absolute altitude: actual height above the terrain/surface (AGL).
  • Pressure altitude: indicated altitude when the altimeter is set to standard pressure, 29.92 inHg; equivalently altitude above the standard datum plane.
  • Density altitude: pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature; operationally it describes the altitude at which the airplane “feels” it is performing in the standard atmosphere.

How the instrument works

  • Inside are sealed aneroid wafers/capsules containing a reference pressure.
  • As outside static pressure decreases in a climb, the wafers expand; as pressure increases in a descent, they contract.
  • Mechanical linkages convert this motion into needle/drum movement.
  • The Kollsman/barometric window adjusts the relationship between sensed pressure and displayed altitude so the instrument can be referenced to the current altimeter setting.

Reading a three-pointer altimeter

  • Traditional altimeters can have separate hands for hundreds, thousands, and ten-thousands of feet. Read the largest-place-value pointer first, then refine with the smaller pointers.
  • Do not read one needle in isolation; the combination determines the altitude.
  • Digital tapes/drums present the same basic pressure-derived information differently.

Altimeter setting

  • Set the latest local pressure setting before takeoff and update it en route as appropriate.
  • With a correct local setting on the ground, indicated altitude should be reasonably close to known airport elevation, within the instrument/system tolerance applicable to the aircraft.
  • Setting 29.92 inHg makes the instrument indicate pressure altitude.

Pressure error — “high to low, look out below”

  • If you fly from an area of higher pressure to lower pressure without resetting the altimeter, true altitude becomes lower than indicated.
  • The airplane follows a lower pressure surface while the instrument still assumes the old higher reference.
  • The opposite pressure change can make true altitude higher than indicated.

Temperature error

  • Altimeters are calibrated to a standard atmosphere. Nonstandard temperature changes the actual spacing of pressure levels.
  • In colder-than-standard air, true altitude can be lower than indicated—another version of “look out below.”
  • Warm air generally spreads pressure levels farther apart, producing the opposite tendency.
  • Temperature error becomes more operationally important when terrain/obstacle clearance margins are small.

Density altitude and performance

  • High pressure altitude and high temperature increase density altitude.
  • High density altitude means lower air density, degrading takeoff, climb, engine, and propeller performance.
  • Density altitude is a performance concept, not simply what the altimeter shows during normal operation.

Static-system failure

  • If the static source is completely blocked, the altimeter tends to freeze at the altitude where blockage occurred.
  • An alternate static source may restore operation with a predictable error; follow the POH/AFM.

Accuracy / terminology note

  • Do not casually use “altitude” to mean AGL. In aviation, charted airport/terrain elevations and normal altimeter indications are generally referenced to MSL; AGL is a separate height-above-surface concept.

What to remember

  • Altimeter = static-pressure aneroid barometer.
  • Know indicated, true, absolute, pressure, and density altitude.
  • 29.92 → pressure altitude.
  • High pressure → low pressure without reset: true altitude lower than indicated.
  • Cold air can also put you lower than indicated.
  • High density altitude = worse aircraft performance.
